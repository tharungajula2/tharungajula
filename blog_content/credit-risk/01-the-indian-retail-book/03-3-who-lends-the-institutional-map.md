# §3 · WHO LENDS — THE INSTITUTIONAL MAP

Product structure is half the picture. The other half is that the same loan carries a different capital cost, a different funding cost and a different regulatory perimeter depending on which licence writes it — and the book moves accordingly.

## 3.1 The five kinds of lender

**Public sector banks.** Lowest funding cost, widest distribution, deepest deposit base. They anchor the large-ticket end of everything: 44.5% of home loan value, 44.6% of gold loan value, 37.2% of auto loan value. Their weakness shows in unsecured — PSU PAR 31–90 in personal loans is the one deteriorating cell in the product.

**Private banks.** Consistently the best asset quality in every product where they compete: home loan PAR 31–90 of 0.87% against 2.41% for PSU banks; personal loan PAR 31–90 of 0.78% against 1.66% for NBFCs; auto at 1.30%. That is borrower selection. They dominate card issuance at 70.7% of new cards, and they are ceding home loan share as they concentrate on prime.

**NBFCs.** The volume engine of Indian retail. 91.3% of personal loan volume, 84.5% of consumer durable value, 70.1% of two-wheeler value, 48.8% of gold loan volume. They compete on speed, reach and underwriting of the undocumented. They pay more for funding, hold more capital against the same assets in some categories, and carry visibly higher delinquency in auto and personal.

**HFCs** — housing finance companies, regulated by the RBI since 2019, having moved across from NHB. Specialists. Lower ticket, informal-income underwriting, tier-3-and-beyond distribution, NHB refinance access, priority-sector classification benefits. Roughly 26% of affordable housing outstanding and 30% of its disbursements.

**SFBs, RRBs, co-operatives and NBFC-MFIs.** Small in aggregate but structurally important at the bottom of the market and disproportionately represented in the microfinance stress cycle.

## 3.2 The rule that explains the whole map

> **Banks own value. NBFCs own volume.**

Check it in every product table in §2 and it holds without exception. The consequences are not cosmetic:

- **Systemic risk lives in the bank book** (value concentration), **but borrower-level distress lives in the NBFC book** (account concentration). A national headline about retail credit health is usually a statement about banks; a statement about household distress is usually a statement about NBFCs.
- **Bureau data and RBI data will therefore disagree in direction**, not just level. The bureau universe includes the NBFC volume; RBI sectoral deployment is banks.
- **A capital rule aimed at banks reshapes the NBFC book** through funding cost, and a rule aimed at NBFCs reshapes the bank book through co-lending. §6 works through the mechanism.

## 3.3 The microfinance cycle — the worked example of a credit cycle

Microfinance is not one of the seven products, but you need it, because it is the only complete boom-stress-recovery cycle available in recent Indian retail data. It is the best single case study for everything Documents 03 through 07 will teach.

**The arc:**

- Gross loan portfolio grew from about ₹2.60 trillion in March 2021 to roughly ₹4.3–4.4 trillion by March 2024, at times growing 27% a year.
- Stress surfaced around June 2024, driven by borrower over-leverage — the same household borrowing from four, five, six lenders — and diluted underwriting during the growth phase.
- The portfolio then **contracted for seven consecutive quarters**, down to around ₹3.25 trillion. NBFC-MFI AUM fell 19% year on year at one point. Disbursements dropped 35% by value and 42% by volume in the worst quarter.
- Of MFIs' opening FY25 loan book, roughly **14.1% was written off during the year**, 3.6% sold to ARCs, 5.4% classified GS3 and 3.4% GS2 — incremental stress of about a quarter of the opening book.
- Sector pre-tax return on assets went to about **−1.7% in FY25**, recovering to roughly −0.1% in FY26.
- The industry imposed **self-regulation through MFIN's guardrails** — borrower indebtedness caps and stricter delinquency norms — which raised rejection rates and deepened the contraction before improving it.
- **Q4 FY26 was the turn.** GLP grew over 3% sequentially to ₹3.25 trillion, the first growth in seven quarters. PAR 31–180 fell to 2.0% from 6.3% a year earlier. Quarterly disbursements of ₹77,524 crore were a seven-quarter high. NBFC-MFIs hold 44.2% of the portfolio, banks 32.7%.
- Recovery is not clean. CRIF's April 2026 data shows **PAR 1–30 ticking back up to 0.8% from 0.6%** even as PAR 1–180 improved to 2.5% — early-bucket stress resurfacing under a headline improvement.

📘 **The five lessons this cycle teaches, which generalise to every unsecured product:**

1. **Over-leverage is a bureau-visible condition, not a borrower characteristic.** The distress came from the same household appearing at multiple lenders. Nobody was individually reckless; the system was.
2. **Write-offs are how a book "improves."** GNPA falling while 14% of the book is written off is not asset quality improving. Always ask for the write-off number alongside the ratio.
3. **The contraction is longer than anyone forecasts.** Lenders repeatedly predicted recovery a quarter or two before it arrived.
4. **Industry self-regulation works and costs growth.** MFIN's guardrails deepened the trough and shortened the cycle.
5. **PAR 1–30 turns first, in both directions.** It is the highest-frequency signal you have.

**► SAY THIS**
> "Microfinance is the cleanest complete credit cycle in recent Indian retail data — boom to March 2024, stress surfacing mid-2024, seven quarters of contraction, and the turn in Q4 FY26 with PAR 31–180 down to 2% from 6.3%. Three things I take from it. The driver was bureau-visible over-leverage rather than individual borrower quality. About 14% of the opening FY25 book was written off, so a lot of the 'improvement' is removal rather than cure. And PAR 1–30 turned before anything else — which is why I'd never manage an unsecured book off the 90-plus number alone."
