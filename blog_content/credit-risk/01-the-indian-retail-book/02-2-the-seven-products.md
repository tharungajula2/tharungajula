# §2 · THE SEVEN PRODUCTS

Each product below follows the same shape: what it is, who lends it, how it is structured, where the risk actually sits, and the line to say.

---

## §2.1 HOME LOANS — the anchor

**₹44.4 lakh crore · +9.4% YoY · 235.7 lakh live accounts · PAR 31–180 of 2.1%**

### What it is

A long-tenor, amortising, mortgage-secured loan against residential property. Twenty-year contractual tenor is standard; observed behavioural life is far shorter because of prepayment, which matters enormously for pricing and for ECL and which Document 05 handles properly.

### Who lends it

Public sector banks are the anchor: **44.5% of Q4 FY26 origination value** and 40.9% of volume. Private banks are ceding share. HFCs took 21.2% of value but 27.4% of volume — value share below volume share, which is the arithmetic tell that they are the lower-ticket lender.

📘 **The value-versus-volume tell.** Whenever a lender's share of origination *value* is lower than its share of *volume*, it is playing at smaller ticket sizes than the market. When value share exceeds volume share, it is playing bigger. This one comparison tells you a lender's strategy without reading a word of their annual report, and it works in every product in this document.

### How it is structured

FY26 originations were ₹11.81 lakh crore across 36.4 lakh loans — value up 12.3%, volume up about 7%. Average ticket size in Q4 FY26 was **₹34.2 lakh**, up 10.6% year on year. Growth in balances is running well ahead of growth in accounts, which is the definition of premiumisation.

By value, the ₹75 lakh-plus band is now 40.7% of originations. By volume, the ₹5–35 lakh band still dominates at 53.7%.

Geographically this is the most metro-concentrated product in the book: **51.8% of origination value comes from the top 8 cities**, only 20.1% from beyond the top 100. The top ten states hold 80.6% of outstanding.

### Where the risk sits

Asset quality is the best in the retail book and improving. PAR 91–180 is 0.2% — effectively nothing. But the composition matters:

- **By lender type (PAR 31–90):** private banks 0.87%, PSU banks 2.41%, HFCs 2.47%, others 2.71%. The spread between private banks and everyone else is borrower selection, not collections.
- **By ticket band (PAR 31–90):** ₹5–35 lakh at 2.85%, ₹35–75 lakh at 1.37%, ₹75 lakh-plus at 0.88%.
- **By state:** Kerala at 4.5% and Andhra Pradesh at 3.8% run well above the 2.1% national PAR 31–180, and Andhra is also growing at the national rate — high growth plus above-average delinquency in the same geography is the combination that should always make you look twice.

⚠️ **The thing nobody says about home loans.** A mortgage book's delinquency is a lagging and flattering indicator during a rising property market, because a distressed borrower with equity sells rather than defaults. Low PAR in housing tells you about collateral values as much as about borrower quality. The test of a mortgage book is the *vintage* curve of the loans written at the top of a price cycle, not the aggregate PAR today.

### The affordable-housing sub-market

You should know this separately because it is where the analytical work is.

Affordable housing is **81.96% of all active housing loan accounts** in India as of H1 FY26. Four out of five mortgage accounts in the country are in this segment. By outstanding value the lender split is roughly: PSU banks 38.55%, private banks around 27%, HFCs 25.83%, NBFCs and others around 6.8% and rising fastest.

But the momentum number is disbursement share, and there **HFCs went from 24.03% in FY21 to 30.12% in H1 FY26** — the fastest of any lender category. Their disbursements beyond tier-3 cities reached 50.29% of their mix, up from 33.10%. Their new-to-credit customer share was 10.51% against 5.69% for banks as of September 2025.

The structural reason is underwriting method, and it is the single most useful thing in this section: **banks underwrite documented income; HFCs underwrite inferred income.** Cash-flow patterns, business vintage, banking behaviour, local market knowledge, physical assessment. That is why they reach borrowers the banking system cannot, and it is also why their books carry a higher and more model-dependent risk.

Funding: bank borrowings are around 65% of affordable HFC liabilities; NHB refinance has held at 16–18% for three years. NHB operationalised **RDCL**, a residential-mortgage securitisation intermediary, in March 2025 with ₹500 crore paid-up capital — a long-run attempt to build a liquid secondary mortgage market. `[VERIFY — check RDCL's issuance volumes to date before citing it as material]`

**► SAY THIS**
> "Housing is a third of retail value and 3% of the accounts, and it's the one product where the aggregate delinquency genuinely is low — PAR 91-plus is around 0.2%. What I'd watch isn't the level, it's two compositional shifts: origination value is migrating hard into the ₹75 lakh-plus band, and origination *volume* is migrating to HFCs underwriting informal income beyond tier-3. Those are two different books being written under one product name, and only one of them is model-light."

## §2.2 GOLD LOANS — the story of FY26

**₹18.6 lakh crore · +50.4% YoY · 899.2 lakh live accounts · PAR 31–180 of 1.2%**

Gold overtook personal loans during FY26 to become the **second-largest retail product after home loans**. Nothing else in the book moved like this.

### Why it moved — four causes, and you must separate them

This is the part that separates a risk analyst from a headline reader. The 50.4% breaks into four distinct drivers, and only one of them is credit demand.

**1. The collateral revalued.** Gold prices ran up sharply. The same pledged jewellery supports a bigger loan. Average ticket size rose **52% year on year to ₹2.19 lakh** while origination volume was essentially flat at around 448 lakh loans in Q4 FY26. Read that again: volumes flat, value nearly doubled. Most of the growth is a price effect, not a borrower effect.

**2. The regulator loosened LTV.** `[IN FORCE]` The **RBI (Lending Against Gold and Silver Collateral) Directions, 2025**, issued 6 June 2025, compliance required no later than **1 April 2026**, replaced the flat 75% loan-to-value cap with a tiered structure:

| Loan amount | Maximum LTV |
|:--|--:|
| Up to ₹2.5 lakh | 85% |
| ₹2.5 lakh to ₹5 lakh | 80% |
| Above ₹5 lakh | 75% |

Alongside: bullet-repayment consumption loans capped at **12 months**; LTV for bullet loans computed on **the total amount repayable at maturity**, not the amount disbursed; **no mandatory credit appraisal** for loans up to ₹2.5 lakh; end-use monitoring compulsory only where the loan is claimed as priority sector; self-declaration accepted where a borrower cannot produce a purchase receipt; pledged gold to be returned within a defined short window after closure.

**3. Reclassification.** Part of the reported growth is agri-gold loans being reclassified into retail. Priority-sector gold loans were **₹6.3 lakh crore of the ₹18.6 lakh crore** at March 2026 — about a third, and that proportion has held roughly steady across the years. If you quote 50.4% without knowing this, someone who does know it will correct you.

**4. Genuine substitution.** With unsecured credit expensive in capital terms and tighter in underwriting, both lenders and borrowers moved to collateral. This is the real credit-behaviour component, and it is smaller than the headline suggests.

🧮 **Worked — why the bullet-repayment LTV change bites.** A one-year bullet loan at 18% against gold valued at ₹1,00,000, under a 75% cap. Old method: disburse ₹75,000, compliant on day one, and at maturity ₹88,500 is owed against ₹1,00,000 of collateral — an effective 88.5% LTV that nobody ever measured. New method: the ₹88,500 repayable is what must sit under 75%, so the lender can disburse only about ₹63,500. The rule did not change the cap. It changed *what the cap is measured against*, and that quietly removed roughly a sixth of the loan on every large bullet ticket. This is why the market is shifting toward EMI structures on bigger tickets.

### Who lends it

**PSU banks 44.6% of Q4 FY26 origination value; NBFCs 31.6%.** But by volume NBFCs are at 48.8% against PSU banks' 32.8%. Apply the value-versus-volume tell: PSU banks own the big tickets, NBFCs own the small ones and are growing share fastest — NBFC value share went from 20.7% in Q4 FY24 to 31.6% in Q4 FY26.

The ticket mix is migrating up hard. Sub-₹1 lakh loans fell from 22.9% to **9.9%** of origination value, and from 63.9% to 45.8% of volume, in two years.

### Where the risk sits

Delinquency is the lowest of the seven products and falling everywhere. NBFC PAR 31–90 collapsed from 2.15% to **0.56%** in a year. PAR 91–180 is 0.20% for NBFCs, 0.05% for PSU banks. Geographically it is a southern product — Tamil Nadu alone holds ₹5.97 lakh crore, with Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Telangana and Kerala behind it — though the fastest growth is now Uttar Pradesh at 78.3%.

⚠️ **The caution that matters more than any of the above.** Falling gold-loan delinquency during a gold price rally is close to meaningless as a credit signal. A borrower whose collateral has appreciated 40% will always find a way to repay or refinance rather than surrender it; and a lender whose collateral has appreciated can auction without loss. **The delinquency improvement is a collateral effect wearing a credit-quality costume.**

The exposure runs the other way. A sustained fall in gold prices puts the loans written near the price peak closest to their collateral value, triggers margin calls, and converts a benign book into a stressed one very quickly. The RBI's **Financial Stability Report of June 2026** flags precisely this: gold-loan growth at a 42.4% CAGR driven substantially by repeat borrowers leveraging higher prices, with a prolonged price correction identified as the delinquency risk. `[IN FORCE — FSR June 2026]`

**► SAY THIS**
> "Gold at 50% growth isn't a credit story, it's four stories. Roughly: collateral revaluation — ticket size up 52% on flat volumes — plus the tiered LTV relaxation to 85% under ₹2.5 lakh, plus agri-gold reclassification into retail, which is about a third of the book, plus genuine substitution away from unsecured. Only the last one is borrower demand. And I'd hold the improving delinquency very lightly, because falling PAR during a bullion rally is a collateral effect, not a credit-quality effect. The number I'd actually monitor is the LTV distribution of the vintage originated near the price peak."

## §2.3 PERSONAL LOANS — the unsecured core

**₹16.5 lakh crore · +12.9% YoY · 1,224.4 lakh live accounts · PAR 31–180 of 2.4% · PAR 180+ of 5.3%**

### What it is

Unsecured, fixed-tenor, no stated end use, no collateral. Everything depends on the underwriting, which means everything depends on the model, which is why this is the product credit-risk teams spend most of their time on despite it being under 10% of the book by value.

### The two personal loan markets

There is no single personal loan product in India. There are two, and they share nothing but a name.

| | Small-ticket | Large-ticket |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Ticket | Under ₹1 lakh | ₹10 lakh and above |
| Share of Q4 FY26 origination **value** | 18.8% | 35.7% |
| Share of Q4 FY26 origination **volume** | 88.6% | 1.8% |
| Who writes it | NBFCs and NBFC-backed fintechs | PSU and private banks |
| PAR 91–180 | 2.27% | 0.33% |

NBFCs took **39.8% of Q4 FY26 origination value and 91.3% of volume.** PSU and private banks together were about 8% of volume and roughly 55% of value.

Nine in ten personal loans written in India are written by an NBFC, and nine in ten are under a lakh. That sentence is the whole product.

Geographically it inverts housing: **41.5% of personal loan origination value comes from beyond the top 100 cities** — the highest BT100 share of any product except consumer durables.

### The number that matters: PAR 180+ at 5.3%

Everything in the personal loan book looks like it is improving. PAR 31–90 is 1.6% and stable. PAR 91–180 improved to 0.8%. And then **PAR 180+ sits at 5.3% and is not moving.**

📘 **What a stable deep bucket beside a falling early bucket means.** Early-bucket delinquency measures *new* stress formation. Deep-bucket delinquency measures *unresolved* stress that hasn't been written off, sold, or recovered. When early buckets fall and the 180+ bucket stays flat, new inflow has slowed but the existing pool is neither curing nor leaving the book. It is the accumulated residue of the 2022–24 origination boom, sitting there.

🔴 **The trap this sets.** If you report improving delinquency on this product using only 31–90 and 91–180, you are reporting the recovery and hiding the damage. Interviewers know this and it is a common probe. The correct framing is: *inflow has normalised, the stock has not resolved.*

### The regulatory shadow

`[IN FORCE]` In November 2023 the RBI raised the risk weight on consumer credit — which is essentially unsecured personal lending, excluding housing, education, vehicle and gold-backed loans — **from 100% to 125% for both banks and NBFCs**. That single change is the reason this product's growth halved between FY24 and FY25 and why FY26's rebound to 28.9% origination growth is treated as significant by everyone in the market.

`[VERIFY]` As at this document's as-at date I find no rollback of the 125% weight on consumer credit. Two partial reversals happened in February 2025 and are covered in §6, but they were about microfinance and about bank exposure to NBFCs, not about consumer credit. Confirm against the current Basel III Master Direction before relying on it.

### What the practitioners are saying

A chief risk officer contributing to the March 2026 CRIF report frames the emerging risk of this product as three things at once: growth sustained at a high rate, concentration in near-prime borrowers, and competitive underwriting pressure from NBFCs and fintechs. He also notes that new-to-credit borrowers are about 15% of retail originations, marginally down year on year, and that demand has become **platform-driven rather than need-driven** — which produces higher velocity and repeat borrowing.

That last phrase deserves attention. A borrower who takes a loan because an app offered it has a different behavioural profile from one who took a loan because they needed it, and a scorecard trained on the second population will misbehave on the first.

### Where the stress is by lender

PSU banks' PAR 31–90 **rose** to 2.14% in March 2026, reversing an improvement, and is now above both private banks (0.78%) and NBFCs (1.66%). That is the one deteriorating cell in an otherwise improving product, and it is exactly the kind of thing you should be able to point at unprompted.

State-wise: Bihar and West Bengal at around 2.9% sit above the 2.4% national PAR 31–180, and Bihar is simultaneously the fastest-growing state at 17.6%. Same pattern as Andhra in housing — the growth-plus-stress combination.

**► SAY THIS**
> "Personal loans are two products. About 89% of the volume is sub-₹1 lakh written by NBFCs and fintechs, and about 36% of the value is ₹10 lakh-plus written by banks. They need separate scorecards, separate cut-offs and separate provisioning assumptions, because the sub-₹1 lakh cohort runs PAR 91–180 around 2.3% against 0.33% for the top band. And the number I'd lead with isn't the improving early bucket — it's PAR 180+ stuck at 5.3%. New inflow has normalised; the stock from the 2022–24 boom hasn't resolved."

## §2.4 AUTO LOANS — the disciplined middle

**₹9.3 lakh crore · +13.9% YoY · 171.6 lakh live accounts · PAR 31–180 of 2.7% · PAR 180+ of 1.9%**

### What it is

Secured against a depreciating asset with an enforceable hypothecation and a functioning repossession market. That combination — real security, but security that loses value faster than the loan amortises in the early years — makes auto the cleanest teaching product for loss given default.

### Structure

FY26 originations of ₹4.06 lakh crore across 47.1 lakh loans, up 12.3% by value and 11.8% by volume, helped materially by GST rationalisation. Average ticket ₹8.64 lakh. The ₹5–10 lakh band is the market: **41% of origination value and 47.5% of volume.** Roughly half of origination value comes from loans above ₹10 lakh.

Q4 FY26 saw a sharp sequential fall — value down 11.6%, volume down 14.2% — which is post-festive normalisation rather than a credit signal. PSU bank originations fell 22.3% in the quarter after two quarters of roughly 30% increases.

⚠️ Indian retail lending has a violent festive seasonality. Q2 and Q3 of the financial year carry disproportionate origination in vehicles, durables and cards. **Never read a Q4 sequential decline in these products as deterioration without checking the same quarter a year earlier.** This is a standard trap in portfolio review meetings.

### Who lends it

PSU banks 37.2% of Q4 FY26 origination value; private banks 27.9%; NBFCs 28.1%. By volume NBFCs lead at 37.4%. Same tell as everywhere: NBFCs downmarket, banks upmarket.

### Where the risk sits

PAR 180+ improved sharply from 3.0% to **1.9%** year on year, which reflects the repossession-and-recovery machinery working. But there is one clear stress cell: **NBFC PAR 31–90 spiked from 1.78% to 3.16%**, against 1.99% for PSU banks and 1.30% for private banks. It eased from 3.43% in December but remains the highest in the product.

Ticket gradient holds: sub-₹5 lakh at 2.95% PAR 31–90 against 1.21% for ₹20 lakh-plus.

**► SAY THIS**
> "Auto is the product where LGD is actually observable, because there's a real repossession and resale market — which is why PAR 180+ halved to 1.9% while other products' deep buckets stayed stuck. The cell I'd flag is NBFC early delinquency, which nearly doubled to 3.16% while banks stayed flat. That's a sourcing-quality question, not a collections question, and I'd want the vintage curves by dealer before saying more."

## §2.5 CREDIT CARDS — the product that stopped

**₹3.4 lakh crore · 0.0% YoY · 1,102.1 lakh cards in circulation · PAR 31–90 of 1.6% · PAR 180+ of 6.9%**

### What happened

Balances flat year on year and down 1.1% sequentially. Cards in circulation grew only 3.3%. And new card issuance has now fallen for two consecutive years: **226.7 lakh in FY25, down 23.0%, then 192.3 lakh in FY26, down a further 15.2%.**

This is a deliberate retreat, and it has a specific cause.

`[IN FORCE]` In November 2023 the RBI raised the risk weight on **credit card receivables from 125% to 150% for scheduled commercial banks**, and from 100% to 125% for NBFCs. Cards are the most capital-expensive retail asset in the Indian system. When capital gets expensive, the marginal account stops being written — and the marginal account in cards is a thin-file, first-time, low-limit customer.

Lenders shifted from open-market acquisition to cross-selling cards into their existing, already-underwritten customer base. Private banks now issue **70.7% of new cards.**

### Where the risk sits

Every early bucket improved: PAR 1–30 down to 2.0%, PAR 31–90 to 1.6%, PAR 91–180 to 1.4%. And **PAR 180+ is 6.9% — the highest deep-bucket number of any product in the retail book.**

Same reading as personal loans, only more so. Improving early buckets on a shrinking, better-selected new-issuance cohort, sitting on top of an unresolved legacy pool from the aggressive 2022–24 acquisition years.

🔴 **The trap specific to cards.** Card delinquency ratios are computed on *balances*, and balances are falling. A flat numerator over a shrinking denominator produces a rising ratio even with zero new stress; a falling numerator over a faster-shrinking denominator can produce a *falling* ratio while absolute losses rise. **In a de-growing revolving book, never analyse the ratio without the absolute rupee amount beside it.** This is the single most reliable place to catch someone who has only read the summary.

### The number conflict you should know about

CRIF's bureau data puts cards in circulation at **1,102.1 lakh — about 110.2 million — as of March 2026.** Widely reported RBI-sourced figures put the base nearer **118.6 million** at the same date. `[VERIFY]`

The gap is definitional, not an error: the two sources treat inactive, blocked, add-on and non-reporting cards differently. Know that the discrepancy exists, cite whichever source you are using, and never split the difference.

**► SAY THIS**
> "Cards are the one product that stopped — balances flat, issuance down 15% after a 23% fall the year before. That's a capital decision, not a demand collapse: at a 150% risk weight, cards are the most expensive retail asset a bank can hold, so the marginal thin-file account stopped being written and acquisition moved to cross-sell inside the existing base. The risk read is that early buckets look great because the new cohort is better selected, but PAR 180+ at 6.9% is the highest deep bucket in retail. And in a shrinking revolving book I'd always put the rupee number next to the ratio."

## §2.6 TWO-WHEELER LOANS — the highest-stress product

**₹1.89 lakh crore · +15.1% YoY · 372.3 lakh live accounts · PAR 31–180 of 4.1% · PAR 360+ of 5.7%**

The smallest of the seven by value and the highest delinquency of the seven. Worth understanding precisely because it is the clearest window onto the bottom of the K.

FY26 originations ₹1.21 lakh crore across 129.9 lakh loans — value up 8.7%, the slowest of the growth products, supported heavily by GST rationalisation and rural cash flows in the festive quarter. Q4 saw a severe post-festive drop: value down 22.1%, volume down 25.6%.

**NBFCs write 70.1% of origination value and 71.4% of volume** — the most NBFC-dominated product after consumer durables, driven by captive financing arrangements with manufacturers, fast underwriting, and reach into segments banks do not serve. Origination is heavily beyond the top 100 cities.

Delinquency improved across every bucket except PAR 360+, which ticked **up** to 5.7%. The stressed band is ₹75,000–₹1 lakh — the commuter motorcycle — at PAR 31–90 of 3.49%, still the worst of any ticket band in the product despite improving from 4.61%.

📘 **Why two-wheeler PAR is structurally high and it is not a scandal.** Small ticket, short tenor, thin-file borrower, seasonal and often informal income, low recovery value on repossession, and a collections economics problem — chasing a ₹3,000 EMI costs nearly as much as chasing a ₹30,000 one. The business is priced for it. A two-wheeler book running 4% PAR 31–180 is not necessarily worse-managed than a mortgage book at 2%; it is a different business with a different loss-and-yield equation. **Compare products to their own history and their own peer set, never to each other.**

**► SAY THIS**
> "Two-wheelers carry the highest PAR in retail at about 4%, and that's structural rather than alarming — small ticket, thin file, informal income, poor recovery value, and collections costs that don't scale down with EMI size. The book is priced for it. What I'd actually flag is that every bucket improved except PAR 360+, which went up. Improving early buckets with a worsening terminal bucket means resolution is failing at the back end even as origination quality improves at the front."

## §2.7 CONSUMER DURABLE LOANS — the front door

**₹1.0 lakh crore · +20.8% YoY · 1,019.3 lakh live accounts · PAR 31–90 of 1.0% · PAR 360+ of 5.2%**

The fastest-growing product after gold, and by some distance the smallest average ticket: **around ₹21,000.**

**NBFCs hold 84.5% of Q4 FY26 origination value and 87.7% of volume**, built on retailer partnerships, point-of-sale financing and BNPL structures. Private banks have fallen to 12.1% of value. FY26 originations were ₹1.78 lakh crore across 897.5 lakh loans, supported by GST rationalisation. Origination skews to beyond the top 100 cities at 42.3% of value.

Its structural importance far exceeds its ₹1 lakh crore of outstanding: **consumer durable finance is the entry point into the formal credit system for first-time borrowers in India.** A no-cost-EMI phone or refrigerator loan is where a thin-file customer acquires a bureau record. Every downstream product's supply of new-to-credit borrowers passes through here.

That makes CD loans a **leading indicator**. Origination volumes and early delinquency in this product move before the same signals appear in personal loans, because the same borrowers arrive here first.

Note the shape: PAR 31–90 of 1.0% is excellent, PAR 360+ of 5.2% is not — the same early-good, deep-bad signature as cards and personal loans, and for the same reason.

**► SAY THIS**
> "Consumer durables is a lakh crore of outstanding and a hundred million accounts at a ₹21,000 ticket, almost entirely NBFC and point-of-sale. Its importance is that it's the front door — it's where new-to-credit borrowers get their first bureau record, so its origination and early-delinquency trends lead personal loans by a couple of quarters. I'd track it as an early-warning series for the unsecured book, not as a book in its own right."
