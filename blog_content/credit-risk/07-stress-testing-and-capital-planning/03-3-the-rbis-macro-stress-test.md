# §3 · THE RBI'S MACRO STRESS TEST

Published twice yearly in the **Financial Stability Report**, and the single best worked example of a stress test available to you — because it is public, current and quantified.

## 3.1 How it is constructed

**Scenarios.** A baseline derived from the RBI's own projections, plus **two adverse scenarios** of increasing severity, built on macro variable paths — GDP growth, inflation, interest rates, exchange rate, current account.

**Transmission.** Macro variables are mapped to bank-level asset quality through fitted relationships, projecting **GNPA ratios, provisions, profitability and capital ratios** forward over a defined horizon.

**Output.** Projected GNPA and CRAR under each scenario, plus the count of banks breaching minimum requirements.

## 3.2 The June 2026 results

From Document 01 §5.1, now read as a stress exercise rather than as portfolio facts:

| Measure | Result |
|:--|:--|
| GNPA, March 2026 actual | **1.8%** — a multi-decadal low |
| GNPA, baseline projection to March 2028 | **1.9%** |
| GNPA, adverse scenarios | **3.8% – 4.1%** |
| Severe credit shock sensitivity | GNPA at **8.1%**; **four banks**, 12% of system assets, breach minimum CRAR |
| NBFCs — 174 entities tested | CRAR **22.8% → 21.7%** baseline, **20.9%** severe; GNPA **2.3% → 5.4%** worst case; **11 NBFCs** below the 15% CRAR requirement |

📘 **Three things to read off this, which is more than most people extract from it.**

**One — the adverse scenarios roughly double GNPA, and the system still holds.** That is the headline finding, and it is genuine.

**Two — the sensitivity analysis is more severe than the scenarios.** GNPA at 8.1% under the single-factor credit shock is far above the 3.8–4.1% of the adverse *scenarios*. Sensitivity analyses are deliberately extreme and are not calibrated to a plausible macro path — which is why comparing a sensitivity result with a scenario result, as commentary often does, mixes two different exercises.

**Three — the NBFC results are worse, and that matters given Document 01 §3.2.** Banks own the value; NBFCs own the volume. Eleven NBFCs falling below the 15% CRAR requirement in the worst case, in a sector that writes 91% of personal loan volume, is a more consequential finding for household credit availability than the four banks.

## 3.3 Top-down and bottom-up

📘 **Top-down** — the regulator applies its own models to supervisory data across all banks. Consistent, comparable, and blunt about individual portfolio composition.

📘 **Bottom-up** — each bank applies the prescribed scenarios to its own portfolio using its own models. Richer, and it exercises the bank's own capability, but comparability suffers and there is an obvious incentive problem.

⚠️ **The honest reading of the incentive problem:** a bank stress-testing itself with its own models has an interest in a benign answer. This is why regulators run top-down exercises alongside bottom-up ones, why the results are compared, and why divergence between the two is itself supervisory information. **A bank whose bottom-up result is consistently more favourable than the regulator's top-down result is telling the supervisor something about its models.**

**► SAY THIS**
> "The FSR stress test is the best public worked example we have. As of June 2026, GNPA at a multi-decadal low of 1.8%, baseline projection 1.9% by March 2028, adverse scenarios 3.8 to 4.1% — so the adverse case roughly doubles it and the system still clears its minimums. The two things I'd draw out that commentary usually misses are that the 8.1% figure is a single-factor sensitivity rather than a scenario, so it isn't comparable to the 4.1%; and that the NBFC results are the more consequential half, because eleven NBFCs breaching in the worst case matters more for household credit availability than four banks, given NBFCs write the overwhelming majority of unsecured retail volume."
