## § 3.2 — Integration archaeology

*This section is not in any AI textbook. It is where 80% of your hours go.*

Rina's extract arrives. 200 anonymised loan files. You open it expecting documents and find an excavation site.

**What's actually in there:**

Roughly 40% native PDFs, generated by the origination system, with real extractable text. These are the easy ones and they lull you.

Roughly 45% **scans** — bank statements faxed by branches, tax transcripts photographed at an angle, a business plan someone printed, signed, scanned, and emailed back. Zero extractable text. These need the vision path from § 2.13.

Roughly 15% miscellaneous: `.doc` files from 2011, three `.msg` email exports, two spreadsheets, and one file that is a PDF containing a screenshot of an Excel table.

And then the specifics that will consume your week:

**Tables come out of PDF extraction as token soup.** A bank statement table extracts as a stream where the column alignment is gone, so `04/12  ACH DEPOSIT  2,400.00  14,220.18` becomes four numbers with no reliable indication of which is the transaction and which is the running balance.

**The schema document is wrong.** The field named `income_verified` contains the *stated* income in about 8% of records, because of a migration in 2019 that nobody documented.

**A FICO of `0` means "no score available," not "worst possible score."** Nobody told you this. You find it because a memo came out saying an applicant had catastrophic credit and Tom laughed.

**The person who knew how the old system worked left in 2019.** There is a wiki page. It was last edited in 2017.

### The FDE moves

**Move 1 — Get the ugliest sample, deliberately, in week one.** Do not accept a curated extract. Ask for *the worst files you have* — the ones people complain about. Your accuracy on clean documents is not information; you already know it'll be high. Your accuracy on a skewed fax is the number that determines whether this ships.

**Move 2 — Find the person, not the document.** The wiki is wrong. Somebody in operations knows why the field flipped in 2019, and that person is usually not in your meetings. Ask Tom who he calls when a file looks weird. Go talk to that person. **This single habit is most of what separates FDEs from consultants** — consultants read the documentation and build to it; FDEs find out what's true.

**Move 3 — Write down every discovered quirk in a shared, versioned file.** `DATA_QUIRKS.md`, in the repo, one entry per surprise: what you expected, what's true, how you found out, what the code does about it.

This does three jobs at once. It's your own memory. It's the handover artifact for the engineer who inherits this in month nine. And when Marcus asks about data quality controls during validation, you hand him a document that already exists — which is a completely different meeting from being asked to produce one.

**Move 4 — Parse quality is a measured number, not an assumption.** Before any embedding happens, sample thirty random extracted pages and read them with your eyes. Count how many are usable. That percentage is the ceiling on everything downstream, and if it's 70%, no amount of clever retrieval will save you.

**THE DEPLOYMENT LENS.** There will come a Tuesday in week seven when you have spent four days on PDF table extraction and have written no AI code at all. You will feel like you are failing.

You are not. **This is the job.** Getting a demo working in a sandbox is 20%; the rest is exactly this. The engineer who finds the 2019 field flip is worth more to this deployment than the one who tunes the reranker, and it is not close.

`[RECEIPT]` **`DATA_QUIRKS.md` with at least fifteen real entries from a genuinely messy corpus.** Nobody self-taught has this artifact, because nobody self-taught works on real customer data. It is disproportionately convincing.
