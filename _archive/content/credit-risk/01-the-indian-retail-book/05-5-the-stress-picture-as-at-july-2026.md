# §5 · THE STRESS PICTURE, AS AT JULY 2026

## 5.1 The system numbers 

| Measure | Value at March 2026 |
|:--|--:|
| Scheduled commercial bank GNPA | **1.8%** — a multi-decadal low |
| Net NPA | 0.5% |
| CRAR | 17.7% — a multi-decadal high |
| CET1 | 15.3% |
| Bank profit after tax, FY26 | ₹4,05,268 crore |
| Baseline projected GNPA, March 2028 | 1.9% |
| Adverse-scenario GNPA | 3.8% – 4.1% |
| Agriculture GNPA | 5.1%, and 37.2% of all gross NPAs |
| Large-borrower share of credit / their GNPA | 44.5% / 1.2% |
| Household debt to GDP | approximately 45.5% |

Sensitivity testing: under the most severe credit shock modelled, GNPA rising to 8.1%, **four banks accounting for 12% of system assets would breach the minimum CRAR.**

NBFCs, stress-tested across 174 entities: aggregate CRAR of 22.8% falling to 21.7% under baseline stress and 20.9% under severe credit stress; GNPA rising from 2.3% to as much as 5.4% in the worst case; **eleven NBFCs failing the 15% CRAR requirement** in the worst outcome.

The RBI's flagged vulnerabilities: rising household debt, rapid gold-loan growth, AI-driven cyber threats and the possibility of an AI-related asset price bubble, alongside the usual global list.

## 5.2 The 1.8% versus 5.3% problem

Here is the apparent contradiction, and it is the highest-value thing in this entire document.

**System GNPA is 1.8%. Personal loan PAR 180+ is 5.3%. Credit card PAR 180+ is 6.9%.**

Both are true simultaneously. Five reasons, and you should be able to give at least four:

**1. Different universes.** GNPA is scheduled commercial banks. Bureau PAR is banks plus NBFCs plus HFCs plus SFBs plus co-operatives — and the unsecured volume sits overwhelmingly in the non-bank part.

**2. Write-offs.** GNPA is measured *after* write-off. A written-off account leaves the GNPA numerator entirely. Bureau records frequently continue to reflect the account as overdue. This is the largest single contributor to the gap, and it is why §3.3's fifth lesson — always ask for the write-off number — matters so much.

**3. Different denominators.** GNPA is over total advances, dominated by corporate and secured retail. PAR 180+ on personal loans is over the personal loan book alone. You are dividing the same kind of numerator by two very different denominators.

**4. Different definitions of the numerator.** NPA is a regulatory classification with prescribed rules — 90 days past due for a term loan, with specific treatment of out-of-order cash credit, upgrades and restructuring. PAR is arithmetic on days past due. They are not the same event.

**5. Product mix.** Retail unsecured is a small share of total advances. A product running 5% deep delinquency inside a category that is 10% of the book contributes 0.5 percentage points to the aggregate, and the aggregate is dominated by a secured corporate and mortgage book that is genuinely clean.

**► SAY THIS**
> "They're not in conflict — they're different instruments measuring different things. GNPA is banks only, net of write-offs, over total advances, using a regulatory 90-day classification. Bureau PAR is the whole lending universe including NBFCs, before write-off adjustment, over the product's own book, on pure days-past-due arithmetic. The gap between them is mostly write-offs and mostly the non-bank book. That's why I'd never assess unsecured retail health off system GNPA — I'd use bureau PAR by product and ticket band, and put the write-off rate next to it."

## 5.3 What is actually deteriorating

Strip the improving headline and four cells are genuinely worth watching:

1. **Personal loan PAR 180+, flat at 5.3%.** Unresolved stock from the origination boom.
2. **Credit card PAR 180+ at 6.9%**, on a shrinking denominator — check the rupee figure.
3. **NBFC auto PAR 31–90, up from 1.78% to 3.16%.** Sourcing quality.
4. **PSU bank personal loan PAR 31–90, up to 2.14%**, reversing an improvement, and now the worst of the three lender types in that product.

And one exposure rather than a deterioration: **the gold book's sensitivity to a bullion price correction**, per §2.2.
