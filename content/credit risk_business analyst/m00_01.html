Every model, regulation, committee, system and job title in this field exists to answer one question about one loan, and then the same question about several million at once.

How much of this money will not come back, and how confident are we in that number?

Two halves, and they are not the same question. The first half is an estimate. The second half is the estimate's reliability — and the second half is where regulatory capital, supervisors and most careers actually live.

This pack is written for the person who has to turn that question into documents a technology team can build from, in a UK bank, under a regulator that reads the documents.

Expected loss reduces to three quantities. Every retail and wholesale credit model in existence is an attempt to estimate one of them.

Expected Loss
PD — probability of defaultThe chance the borrower stops paying, over a stated horizon. A number between 0 and 1
×
LGD — loss given defaultThe share not recovered afterwards, after collateral, collections and costs. Also 0 to 1
×
EAD — exposure at defaultHow much is actually owed at the moment it fails, including undrawn limits likely to be drawn
=
EL — expected lossThe amount expected to be lost, in currency

A £1,000,000 facility with a 2% PD, 45% LGD and full drawdown gives an expected loss of £9,000. That is not a prediction that this loan loses £9,000 — it either defaults or it does not. It is what a very large book of identical facilities would average.

Learn these three letters properly. In a Basel or Moody's environment they appear in every requirement document, every data mapping and every test case you will ever write.
`}, {l:"WHY",t:"Expected and unexpected",h:"The distinction that explains everything",b:`

Expected loss is the average. Actual losses vary around that average, year to year, and in a bad year they land far above it.

Expected lossThe average, foreseeable cost of lending. It is a cost of doing business. You price for it and you provide for it.
Unexpected lossThe amount by which a bad year exceeds the average. You cannot price for it because you do not know when it lands. You hold capital against it.

This single distinction produces the entire architecture of bank regulation:

  • Provisions absorb expected loss and sit in the profit and loss account. Accounting owns them.
  • Capital absorbs unexpected loss and sits on the balance sheet as shareholders' money. The regulator owns the rules.
  • Stress testing asks how large unexpected can plausibly get.
If someone in a meeting conflates provisions and capital, they are conflating the average with the tail. It is the single most common conceptual error in the field.
`}, {l:"DEFINE",t:"Default",h:"A defined event, not an opinion",b:`

Everything above depends on a precise definition of the word default. Basel supplies one, and it has two limbs. Either triggers default.

190 days past due — the obligor is more than 90 days late on any material credit obligation to the group
2Unlikeliness to pay — the bank judges the obligor unlikely to pay in full without recourse to actions such as realising security

The second limb is judgemental and is where banks differ most. Distressed restructuring, sale of the obligation at a material credit-related loss, bankruptcy filing and specific credit-risk adjustments all count.

The definition matters to you because it is a data requirement. Someone has to source days-past-due, materiality thresholds, forbearance flags and write-off dates, and reconcile them across systems. That someone is usually the BA.

Official source Basel Framework CRE36 — IRB minimum requirements, including the definition of default
`}, {l:"PICTURE",t:"The loss distribution",h:"One curve behind every rule",b:`

Plot the losses a large loan book could produce in a year. You get a curve with a fat right tail: most years cluster near the average, a few are catastrophic.

Where each concept sits on the curve
Expected lossThe mean of the distribution. Covered by provisions and by pricing.
Unexpected lossThe distance from the mean out to a chosen confidence point. Covered by regulatory capital.
The 99.9% pointThe confidence level Basel uses for credit risk capital. One bad year in a thousand.
Beyond the tailNot capitalised. This is what stress testing, recovery planning and resolution exist for.

Read that table again in a week. Almost every argument you will hear in a capital meeting is an argument about where on this curve a particular thing belongs.

`}, {l:"DEFINE",t:"RWA",h:"The denominator of everything",b:`

Risk-weighted assets (RWA) is a bank's asset base restated by risk rather than by size. A £1m gilt holding and a £1m unsecured personal loan are the same number on the balance sheet and wildly different numbers in RWA.

Every asset gets a risk weight, a percentage. Exposure multiplied by risk weight equals RWA.

Cash / central bank0%
UK government bonds0%
Residential mortgage (low LTV)~20%
Corporate, investment grade~50-75%
Unsecured retail~75%
Unrated corporate / speculative100%+

Capital ratios are then capital divided by RWA. Which means every conversation about "capital" is really a conversation about the denominator, and every conversation about the denominator is really a conversation about data: exposure values, ratings, collateral, maturities, currencies.

Indicative weights only, to build intuition. The exact weights come from the standardised approach tables or from a bank's own IRB models, both covered in Mission 05.

`}, {l:"DEFINE",t:"Three lines of defence",h:"Who is allowed to say no",b:`

UK banks are organised around a governance model you will hear referenced constantly. It determines who can challenge whom.

1First line — the business. Originates the risk and owns it. Front office, lending, treasury dealing.
2Second line — risk and compliance. Sets the framework, independently measures and challenges the first line. Credit risk, market risk, model validation.
3Third line — internal audit. Independently assures that lines one and two work. Reports to the board audit committee.

Change programmes sit awkwardly across this. A BA typically works for a programme sponsored by second line or by Finance, gathering requirements from first line, delivering through Technology, and producing artefacts that third line and the regulator will later inspect.

Practical consequence: your BRD is an audit artefact. It will be read years later by someone looking for evidence that a decision was deliberate. Write it accordingly.
`}, {l:"TRAP",t:"Model risk",h:"The number is not the truth",b:`

PD, LGD and EAD are estimates produced by models fitted to history. Three failure modes recur, and all three land on the BA's desk as data problems.

The data was wrongMissing collateral values, stale ratings, duplicated exposures, unmapped legal entities. The model is fine; the input is not.
History stopped applyingThe model learned a relationship that a rate cycle, a pandemic or a regulation broke. Performance decays quietly.

The third is the dangerous one: the number was correct and nobody could show why. If you cannot trace a reported figure back through every transformation to its source system, the number is unusable to a supervisor regardless of accuracy. That principle has a name and a rulebook, and it is Mission 10.
