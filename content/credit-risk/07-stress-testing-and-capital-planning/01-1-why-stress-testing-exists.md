# §1 · WHY STRESS TESTING EXISTS

## 1.1 The gap that provisions do not cover

Document 06 §3 established that ECL is a **probability-weighted** expectation across scenarios. That is the right way to compute an expected value. It is the wrong way to answer a survival question.

📘 **The distinction, stated as cleanly as I can put it:**

- **ECL** takes the downside scenario and multiplies it by 30%. It answers *what do we expect to lose on average?*
- **A stress test** takes the downside scenario and asks *what if this simply happens?* — weight 100%, no averaging.

Both are correct for their purpose. **Provisions absorb the expected; capital absorbs the unexpected; a stress test sizes the unexpected.**

🧮 Return to Document 06 §3.2's numbers. Probability-weighted ECL was ₹121 crore. But the downside scenario alone produced **₹180 crore**. The ₹59 crore difference between the provision you carry and the loss you would actually suffer **is what capital is for.** That single comparison is the cleanest available explanation of why a bank needs both, and it is worth being able to draw.

## 1.2 The three kinds of exercise

| Type | Question | Direction |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Sensitivity analysis** | What if this one variable moves? | One factor at a time |
| **Scenario analysis** | What if this coherent set of conditions occurs? | Many factors, internally consistent |
| **Reverse stress testing** | What would it take to break us? | Starts from the failure and works backwards |

⚠️ Sensitivity analysis is easy and mostly uninformative, because real stress arrives as correlated movements, not as one variable moving alone. Scenario analysis is the standard. **Reverse stress testing is the most useful and the least practised**, and §5 argues why.
