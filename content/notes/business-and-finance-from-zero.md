# 1. Where the money is

Every business, anywhere, gets described by three documents. Not four, not seven. Three. And they were designed to be read together, which is why people who half-understand them find them confusing — a single statement on its own genuinely doesn't tell you much.

Here they are, one at a time.

## The first: what happened over a period

The **income statement** — also called the **profit and loss statement**, or just the **P&L** — answers one question: over some stretch of time, did we make money?

It runs top to bottom like a subtraction problem.

You start with **revenue**, the money customers paid you. Then you take off **cost of goods sold**, usually shortened to **COGS**, which is the direct cost of the thing you sold — the steel in the car, the electricity in the charge, the cloud hosting for the customer's account. What's left is **gross profit**.

From gross profit you subtract **operating expenses** — salaries, rent, marketing, legal, everything that keeps the lights on but isn't directly attached to a unit sold. What's left is **operating profit**, which in finance is almost always called **EBIT**, standing for *earnings before interest and tax*.

Then two more subtractions. **Interest**, which is what you pay lenders. And **tax**. What survives all of that is **net income** — sometimes called the bottom line, because it is literally the bottom line.

Revenue at the top, net income at the bottom, and a series of costs peeled off in between. That's it.

## The second: what you own and owe, right now

The **balance sheet** is not about a period. It's a photograph taken at one instant — usually the last day of a quarter or a year.

It has two sides, and they are always equal:

**Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

**Assets** are things the business owns or is owed — cash, machinery, buildings, inventory sitting in a warehouse, and money customers owe you but haven't paid yet. That last one is called **accounts receivable**, or just *receivables*.

**Liabilities** are what the business owes to others — bank loans, bonds, and money you owe suppliers but haven't paid yet, which is called **accounts payable**.

**Equity** is what's left for the owners after every liability is settled. It's a residual, not a thing you can point at.

The equation always balances because it's a tautology, not a discovery. Everything the business has, someone has a claim on. Either an outsider (liability) or an owner (equity). There is no third option.

## The third: where the cash actually went

Here is the thing that confuses everyone at first: **a company can be profitable and still run out of money.**

It happens constantly. You sell ₹1 crore of goods in March. Profit looks great. But your customers pay in June, your suppliers demanded payment in February, and in April you cannot make payroll. The P&L said you were fine. The bank account said otherwise.

The **cash flow statement** exists to close that gap. It tracks actual rupees in and out, split into three buckets:

**Operating** — cash from running the business day to day.
**Investing** — cash spent buying long-lived things, or received from selling them.
**Financing** — cash from raising debt or equity, and cash going out to repay debt or pay dividends.

It starts from net income (the bottom of the P&L) and then adjusts, because net income contains things that were never cash.

## The one adjustment you must understand

The biggest of those non-cash items is **depreciation**.

Say you buy a machine for ₹100 lakh. It'll last ten years. Accounting doesn't let you record a ₹100 lakh cost in year one, because that would make year one look catastrophic and years two through ten look artificially wonderful, when really you were using the machine the whole time.

So instead you spread it: ₹10 lakh of cost in each of ten years. That annual ₹10 lakh is **depreciation**.

The word for the same idea applied to intangible things — software, patents, brand value — is **amortisation**. Together they're written **D&A**.

The critical fact: **depreciation is not cash.** The cash left the building on day one when you bought the machine. In year four, depreciation reduces your reported profit by ₹10 lakh but not one rupee moves.

Two related terms while we're here. **Capex** — short for *capital expenditure* — is the money you spend buying long-lived assets. Buying the machine is capex. And **PP&E**, standing for *property, plant and equipment*, is where those assets sit on the balance sheet.

## The question they will ask you

This is the most-asked question in finance interviews, and it exists precisely because it tests whether you understand that the three statements are one system.

*"Depreciation goes up by 10. Walk me through all three statements."*

Assume tax is 40%.

**Income statement.** Depreciation is an expense, so it rises by 10 and pre-tax profit falls by 10. But you now owe less tax — 40% of 10, so 4 less. Net income falls by **6**, not 10.

**Cash flow statement.** Start from net income, down 6. Now add back the full 10 of depreciation, because it never was cash. Net effect: **cash is up 4.**

Read that again. Depreciation *increased your cash.* Not because depreciation generates money, but because it reduced your tax bill by 4, and tax is very much cash. This is called the **tax shield**, and it is the whole reason the question is asked.

**Balance sheet.** Cash is up 4. PP&E is down 10, because the asset depreciated. Assets net down 6. On the other side, retained earnings — the accumulated profits sitting inside equity — are down 6, because net income was down 6.

Down 6 on both sides. It balances. It always balances.

## Three ways of saying "profit," and when to use each

You'll hear three measures thrown around, and people substitute them carelessly. They're not the same thing.

**Net income** is profit after literally everything — costs, interest, tax, depreciation. It's the truest measure of what the owners earned, but it's also the easiest to distort, because depreciation schedules and tax structures vary wildly between companies.

**EBITDA** — *earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation* — strips those distortions out. Take EBIT and add back D&A. What you're left with is a rough proxy for how much cash the core operation throws off, independent of how the company is financed or how aggressively it depreciates. It's the standard yardstick for comparing two businesses.

**Free cash flow** is operating cash flow minus capex. This is the real money left over after keeping the business running.

The trap: **EBITDA flatters capital-heavy businesses badly**, because it ignores capex entirely. A company that has to spend ₹15 lakh on a machine every few years to earn anything can show handsome EBITDA and generate no free cash whatsoever. Hold that thought — it comes back hard in Chapter 9.

## One more concept: the cash trapped inside operations

**Working capital** is current assets minus current liabilities — meaning, in practice, receivables plus inventory minus payables.

Think of it as the money stuck inside your own operating cycle. You've bought the raw material, you've made the thing, you've shipped it, but the customer hasn't paid. That's cash you've spent and not recovered.

Growth makes this worse, not better. If you double sales, you roughly double the cash trapped in receivables and inventory. **A fast-growing business can be strangled by its own success.** This is not a rare pathology. It's one of the most common ways good companies die.

---

**Hold on to three things.** Assets equal liabilities plus equity, always. Non-cash expenses reduce profit but raise cash by the tax they save. And profit is not cash — the gap between them is where companies die.

# 2. Building a model

A **financial model** is a spreadsheet in which the three statements from Chapter 1 are wired together, so that changing one assumption ripples through everything downstream.

That's the whole definition. It's not a forecast, exactly — it's a machine for asking *what if*.

## The only distinction that matters

Every cell in a model is one of two things.

A **driver** is an assumption you control. Revenue growth rate. Gross margin. Number of salespeople. How many days customers take to pay. Drivers are typed in by a human.

An **output** is anything the model calculates. Revenue. Profit. Cash. Outputs are formulas, never typed.

**Every driver lives on one sheet, clearly labelled, in one place.** Nowhere else in the model does a human-typed number appear. If someone asks "what if growth were 15% instead of 10%," you change one cell and the entire model responds.

Get this right and you have a model. Get it wrong and you have a spreadsheet full of numbers that nobody, including you, will ever be able to change safely.

## The conventions, which are also the grading criteria

These aren't aesthetic preferences. They're how anyone who works with models judges yours in about ten seconds.

**Blue for typed inputs, black for formulas.** Universal, and its absence is the first thing noticed.

**Never bury a number inside a formula.** `=B5*1.1` is a defect — the 1.1 is invisible and unchangeable. `=B5*(1+$C$2)` with the growth rate sitting in cell C2 is correct. (The dollar signs *anchor* the reference so it doesn't drift when you copy the formula sideways.)

**One row, one calculation, carried consistently across every period.** If January's formula differs from February's, something is wrong.

**A balance check.** One row that computes assets minus liabilities minus equity. It must read zero. If it doesn't, you have a bug, and you find it before you show anyone.

## The circular reference, and why it isn't a bug

Here's a loop that traps everyone the first time.

Interest expense depends on how much debt you have. How much debt you have depends on whether you had enough cash. Cash depends on net income. And net income depends on interest expense.

The model is chasing its own tail. This is a **circular reference**, and Excel will complain about it.

It's not an error. It's the actual structure of the problem. The standard fix is a switch cell that breaks the loop when you need it broken, plus turning on Excel's **iterative calculation** setting, which lets it solve the loop by repeated approximation instead of giving up.

Knowing this exists — and knowing it's normal rather than something you did wrong — is itself a signal of competence.

## A model, built small

Here's the smallest complete thing.

Revenue last year was 100. Your growth driver says 10%, so this year is **110**.

COGS runs at 60% of revenue — that's a driver too. So COGS is 66, and gross profit is **44**.

Operating expenses are 20, held flat. EBIT is **24**.

Interest is 4, which comes from your debt schedule. Pre-tax profit is **20**.

Tax at 25% is 5. **Net income is 15.**

Now that 15 does two more jobs. It opens the cash flow statement, where you add back D&A, adjust for the change in working capital, and subtract capex — landing on how much cash you actually generated. And it flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet, increasing equity by 15.

Change the growth driver from 10% to 15% and every one of those numbers moves. That's a model.

## How to build one in three hours

You will, at some point, be handed a company and a deadline. Here's the sequence.

Start with the **driver sheet**. Nothing else. List every assumption: customers, price per customer, growth rate, cost percentages, headcount and salaries, capex. Get them all in one place before you write a single formula.

Then the **revenue build**. Not one line called "revenue" — separate lines for each way the company earns, each driven by its own assumptions, summing to a total.

Then the **cost build**. Salaries from a headcount table, so that adding a person is a real change with a real number attached. Everything else after.

Then **cash**. Opening cash, monthly burn, closing cash, and a row that computes runway.

Then a **scenario switch** — one cell, set to "base," "upside" or "downside," that changes three or four drivers together. This is the single feature that turns a model from a report into a tool, because it lets someone ask a question and get an answer in one keystroke.

Then the **balance check**.

## The errors that get spotted instantly

Hardcoded numbers inside formulas. Links that broke when someone inserted a row. Signs that flip — costs positive in one place, negative in another. No balance check. No units labelled, so nobody knows if 15 means fifteen rupees, fifteen lakh, or fifteen crore. And circularity left unresolved, with `#REF!` errors sitting on the page.

Each of these is fifteen seconds of work to prevent and instant credibility damage to leave in.

---

**Hold on to three things.** Drivers on one sheet, formulas everywhere else. The circular reference between interest, debt and cash is normal, not a mistake. And a scenario switch is what makes a model useful rather than decorative.

# 3. What a business is worth

There are only three ways to value a business, and they're all versions of the same two ideas: *what are similar things selling for*, and *what cash will this thing produce*.

But before any of that, you need one distinction absolutely straight, because getting it wrong is the fastest way to lose a room.

## Enterprise value versus equity value

**Equity value** is what the shareholders own. For a listed company it's simply the share price times the number of shares, which is called **market capitalisation** or *market cap*.

**Enterprise value** is the value of the whole operating business — to everyone who financed it, shareholders and lenders alike.

They're related like this:

**Enterprise value = Equity value + Debt − Cash**

(There are two more items in the full formula — *preferred stock*, which is a class of shares that ranks ahead of ordinary shares, and *minority interest*, which is the slice of a subsidiary the parent doesn't own. For most purposes you can leave them out.)

The intuition is simple and worth internalising rather than memorising. Imagine buying the company outright. You pay the shareholders for their shares. But you also inherit the company's debt, which you'll have to repay — so that's a real additional cost. And you get the company's cash, which you can immediately use to pay down some of what you just spent — so that's a discount.

Add debt. Subtract cash. That's enterprise value.

Run it backwards and you get the version you'll use more often:

**Equity value = Enterprise value − Net debt**

where **net debt** is simply debt minus cash.

Why does anyone care? Because when you compare two companies, one might be loaded with debt and the other debt-free. Their equity values could be wildly different while the underlying businesses are identical. Enterprise value lets you compare the businesses. Equity value tells you what the shares are worth.

## Method one: what are similar companies trading at

This is **comparable company analysis**, universally called **trading comps**.

You find listed companies that look like yours — same industry, similar size, similar growth. You look at the multiple they trade at. You apply it to your company.

The workhorse multiple is **EV/EBITDA** — enterprise value divided by EBITDA. It's popular precisely because both halves are financing-neutral, so it compares operations rather than balance sheets.

Worked: your peers trade at 10 times EBITDA. Your target's EBITDA is ₹50 crore.

Enterprise value = 10 × 50 = **₹500 crore**.

The target has ₹100 crore of net debt. So equity value = 500 − 100 = **₹400 crore**.

That's the whole method. Its strength is that it reflects what the market will actually pay today. Its weakness is that it inherits whatever mood the market is in.

## Method two: what have buyers actually paid

**Precedent transaction analysis** is the same arithmetic, but instead of what similar companies *trade* at, you use what buyers have actually *paid* in past acquisitions.

These multiples run higher than trading comps, reliably. The reason is the **control premium** — a buyer taking control of a whole company pays more per share than an investor buying a 0.1% slice, because control lets you change management, cut costs, and redirect strategy. That optionality has value.

## Method three: what cash will it produce

The **discounted cash flow**, or **DCF**, is the only method that values the business on its own merits rather than by comparison.

The logic: a business is worth the cash it will generate in future, but future cash is worth less than cash today. So project the cash, then shrink each future year back to present-day terms.

Three pieces.

**First, the discount rate.** What return do the people who funded this business require? That's the **WACC** — *weighted average cost of capital*:

**WACC = (E/V) × Re + (D/V) × Rd × (1 − tax)**

E is equity, D is debt, V is E plus D. Re is the cost of equity, Rd the cost of debt. Debt gets multiplied by (1 − tax) because interest payments are tax-deductible, so debt is cheaper than its headline rate.

To get the cost of equity you use **CAPM** — the *capital asset pricing model*:

**Re = Rf + β × ERP**

**Rf** is the **risk-free rate**, the return on government bonds, taken as the safest thing available. **β** (beta) measures how violently a stock moves relative to the market — beta of 1 means it moves with the market, 1.5 means it exaggerates every swing by half. **ERP** is the **equity risk premium**, the extra return investors demand for holding shares instead of bonds.

Worked: risk-free rate 7%, beta 1.2, equity risk premium 6%.

Re = 7% + (1.2 × 6%) = **14.2%**.

**Second, project the cash.** Usually five years of free cash flow, built from a model like Chapter 2's. Discount each year back: a cash flow in year three gets divided by (1 + WACC) cubed.

**Third, the terminal value.** The business doesn't stop in year five. **Terminal value** captures everything after your forecast, and — this surprises people — it's usually well over half the total answer.

Two ways to get it. The **perpetuity growth** method assumes the business grows forever at some modest rate:

**TV = FCF × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g)**

Worked: final-year free cash flow 100, growth 4%, WACC 12%.

TV = 100 × 1.04 ÷ (0.12 − 0.04) = 104 ÷ 0.08 = **1,300**.

Notice how sensitive that is. Push growth to 5% and the denominator becomes 0.07, giving 1,500 — a 15% swing from a one-point change in an assumption nobody can actually know. This is why DCFs are argued about.

The alternative, the **exit multiple** method, applies an EV/EBITDA multiple to the final forecast year, borrowing from Method one.

Discount the terminal value back too, add it to the discounted cash flows, and you have enterprise value. Subtract net debt for equity value.

## Presenting all three

You never present one number. You present a **football field** — a chart with one horizontal bar per method, each showing the range that method implies.

Leadership then sees where the three overlap. That overlap is the answer. A single point estimate implies a precision that doesn't exist and invites an argument about the wrong thing.

## When none of this applies

All three methods assume a business with a track record.

Early-stage companies don't have one. A DCF on a company with twelve customers is arithmetic theatre — you're compounding guesses for five years and then applying a terminal value to a number you invented.

Early-stage valuation is set by negotiation: the strength of the team, whatever traction exists, the size of the market, and how many investors are competing for the round.

Learn the DCF because you will be tested on it. But know that saying *"this is the wrong tool for a company at this stage, and here's what actually sets the price"* is the better answer, and a rarer one.

---

**Hold on to three things.** Enterprise value is equity plus debt minus cash; equity value is enterprise value minus net debt. Terminal value usually carries most of a DCF and rests on assumptions nobody can verify. And knowing when a tool doesn't apply beats knowing the tool.

# 4. How businesses get bought

Short chapter. You'll be asked about this and you won't use it. Learn the shape and the two calculations.

## The process

A company decides to sell, and hires a bank.

The bank writes a **teaser** — one or two anonymous pages describing the business well enough to interest a buyer without identifying it — and circulates it.

Interested buyers sign an **NDA**, a *non-disclosure agreement*, promising not to leak what they're about to see. Then they receive the **CIM** — the *confidential information memorandum* — the full document with financials, customers and strategy.

Buyers submit first-round bids. The serious ones get invited to management presentations and into the **data room**, an online repository holding every contract, audit and employment agreement. They spend weeks on **due diligence**, which is the buyer's investigation into whether the business is what the seller says it is.

Then final bids, signing, regulatory approval, and closing.

Some vocabulary while you're here. A **pitch book** is the deck a bank uses to win the work in the first place. A **mandate** is the formal engagement once they've won it. **League tables** rank banks by deal volume, which is how the industry keeps score. **Sell-side** means advising the seller; **buy-side** means the investors doing the buying. **Bulge bracket** means the global giants — Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan. **Boutiques** are smaller specialist advisory firms like Rothschild, Moelis and Evercore.

## Why deals happen, and why they fail

The stated reason is usually **synergies** — value created by combining. **Cost synergies** are removing duplicated overhead: one finance team instead of two. These are real and usually achieved. **Revenue synergies** are selling each company's products to the other's customers. These are usually promised and rarely delivered.

When you pay more than the fair value of what you bought, the excess sits on your balance sheet as **goodwill**. It's an accounting plug. When deals go wrong, goodwill gets written off, and that write-off is the public admission that you overpaid.

## Accretion and dilution

The first question an acquirer's board asks: does this deal make our earnings per share go up or down?

**EPS** — *earnings per share* — is net income divided by the number of shares outstanding.

Worked. The acquirer earns ₹100 and has 100 shares. EPS is **₹1.00**.

It buys a target that earns ₹20, paying with 15 newly issued shares.

Combined earnings: 120. Combined shares: 115.

New EPS = 120 ÷ 115 = **₹1.043**.

EPS went up, so the deal is **accretive**. Had it fallen, the deal would be **dilutive**.

(A real analysis also subtracts the cost of financing and adds expected synergies. The mechanic is the same: combine the earnings, combine the share count, compare.)

## Leveraged buyouts

A **leveraged buyout**, or **LBO**, is when a **private equity** firm — an investment firm that buys whole companies rather than shares — acquires a business using mostly borrowed money, improves it, and sells it in roughly five years.

The debt is repaid out of the acquired company's own cash flows. That's the trick: you borrow against the target's ability to pay, not your own.

Why bother? Because leverage magnifies the equity return. Buy a ₹1,000 company with ₹600 of debt and ₹400 of your own equity. If you sell for ₹1,600 having paid down ₹200 of debt, the remaining debt is ₹400 and your equity is worth ₹1,200. You tripled your money on a company that appreciated 60%.

Two ways to state that return.

**MOIC** — *multiple on invested capital* — is exit equity divided by entry equity. Here, 1,200 ÷ 400 = **3.0 times**.

**IRR** — *internal rate of return* — is the annualised version. Three times over five years is roughly **25% a year**, because 1.25 to the power of five is about 3.05.

Worth memorising that pairing: **3× over 5 years ≈ 25% IRR**. It comes up constantly and being able to do it instantly is a small, cheap credibility win.

## Going public

An **IPO** — *initial public offering* — is a company selling shares to the public for the first time.

In India: hire banks, do diligence, file a **DRHP** (*draft red herring prospectus*) with **SEBI**, the Securities and Exchange Board of India, which regulates the capital markets. SEBI reviews it. The company does a roadshow, meeting investors. Then **book-building** — collecting how much investors will buy at what price, which sets the final price. Then listing.

The teams that do this sit in **capital markets**, split into **ECM** (equity capital markets — share issues) and **DCM** (debt capital markets — bonds and loans).

India, as it stands, is the world's busiest IPO market by number of listings. It's also become noticeably more selective since 2025 — investors now weight profitability and post-listing performance over the first-day pop.

---

**Hold on to three things.** Accretive means combined EPS rises; combine the earnings, combine the shares, compare. Three times over five years is about 25% IRR. And revenue synergies are usually promised, rarely delivered.

# 5. How startups get funded

Everything in Part One assumed a company with history. Startups don't have history, so the machinery is different.

## The ladder

**Pre-seed** is an idea and a team. **Seed** is a first product and early signs someone wants it. **Series A** is the round you raise when you've found **product-market fit** — the point where customers pull the product from you rather than being pushed into it. **Series B and C** fund scaling something already proven. **Growth** rounds fund expansion, usually with an eye on eventual listing.

Early rounds fund the *search*. Later rounds fund the *scaling*. Confusing the two is how companies burn money.

## Pre-money, post-money, and the arithmetic of dilution

Two words that must never be mixed up.

**Pre-money valuation** is what the company is agreed to be worth *before* the new money arrives. **Post-money** is what it's worth *after*.

**Post-money = Pre-money + Investment**

And the investor's ownership is:

**Investor % = Investment ÷ Post-money**

Worked. Founders hold 8,000,000 shares — 100% of the company.

A Series A investor puts in ₹2 crore at a ₹10 crore pre-money valuation. Post-money is 10 + 2 = **₹12 crore**.

Investor ownership = 2 ÷ 12 = **16.7%**.

Founders are diluted from 100% to **83.3%**.

Note what happened: the founders didn't sell anything. Their share count is unchanged. New shares were issued, so their slice of a bigger pie is smaller. That's **dilution**.

## The employee pool, and the trick hidden inside it

Companies reserve shares for future employees. That reserve is the **ESOP pool** — *employee stock option pool*. Typically it's topped up at each round.

Here's where it gets interesting. Investors almost always insist the pool be created *before* the round, out of the pre-money valuation.

Continue the example. Add a 15% ESOP pool, created pre-money.

The founders' 83.3% drops to roughly **68.3%**. The investor still holds 16.7%. The pool holds 15%.

The founders absorbed the entire pool. The investor's percentage didn't move.

Had the pool been created *after* the round, out of post-money, the dilution would have been shared between founders and investor proportionally.

**This is the cap-table question that separates people who understand this from people who've read about it.** When someone shows you a term sheet, ask: is the option pool in the pre-money or the post-money? The answer moves founder ownership by several percentage points and quietly changes the effective price the investor paid.

Ask the same question about any converting instruments — which brings us to those.

## SAFEs and convertible notes

Pricing a company is slow and expensive. Early investors often skip it.

A **SAFE** — *simple agreement for future equity* — is a contract: the investor gives money now, and gets shares later, when the next priced round happens. It isn't debt. There's no interest and no repayment date.

A **convertible note** does the same job but is legally structured as short-term debt that converts to equity.

Both usually carry two sweeteners.

A **valuation cap** sets a maximum valuation at which the money converts. If the investor put in at a ₹10 crore cap and the next round prices at ₹40 crore, they convert as though the company were worth ₹10 crore — four times the shares. That's their reward for going early.

A **discount** gives them a percentage off the next round's price, typically 20%.

The thing to remember: **these defer pricing, not dilution.** The dilution is real, it's just invisible until conversion. Founders who've raised on several SAFEs are sometimes shocked at their ownership when the Series A finally prices.

## The clauses that decide who actually gets paid

A **term sheet** is the outline of a deal. Four clauses matter more than the rest.

**Liquidation preference** governs who gets paid first if the company is sold. "1× non-participating" means the investor takes back their money first, or converts to ordinary shares and takes their percentage — whichever is better, but not both. That's founder-friendly and standard. "Participating" means they take their money back *and then also* their percentage of what's left. "2× participating" means double their money back first, then their percentage. In a mediocre exit, aggressive preferences can leave founders with nothing while the headline sale price looks respectable.

**Anti-dilution** protects the investor if a later round prices *lower* than theirs — a **down round**. Their shares get repriced, and the dilution lands on the founders.

**Pro-rata rights** let the investor put in more money at the next round to maintain their percentage.

**Vesting** applies to founders and employees alike. The standard is four years with a **one-year cliff**: you get nothing if you leave inside twelve months, then it accrues monthly for the remaining three years.

---

**Hold on to three things.** Post-money is pre-money plus investment; investor percentage is investment over post-money. SAFEs defer pricing, not dilution. And whether the option pool sits pre-money or post-money is the question to ask every single time.

# 6. How startups get measured

A startup with no profit still has to be judged on something. Over the last fifteen years the industry converged on a fairly tight set of measures. Know them cold — they come up in every conversation, and using them precisely marks you out.

Every number below is illustrative, chosen so the arithmetic is clean.

## Revenue that repeats

**MRR** is *monthly recurring revenue* — subscription revenue that will arrive again next month without anyone selling anything. **ARR** is *annual recurring revenue*, which is simply MRR times twelve.

Worked: 1,000 customers paying ₹5,000 a month gives MRR of ₹50 lakh, so **ARR is ₹6 crore**.

The word *recurring* is load-bearing. A one-off implementation fee is revenue, but it isn't ARR, and quietly including it is one of the more common forms of startup number-inflation.

## Margin

**Gross margin** is (revenue minus COGS) divided by revenue.

Worked: ₹100 of revenue, ₹25 of COGS, gives **75%**.

Software businesses typically run 70–80%. Hardware and asset businesses run far lower, which changes everything downstream.

## Burn and runway

**Gross burn** is total cash going out each month. **Net burn** is cash out minus cash in — the actual rate at which the bank balance falls.

Worked: ₹40 lakh out, ₹25 lakh in. Net burn is **₹15 lakh a month**.

**Runway** is cash divided by net burn — how many months until zero.

Worked: ₹3 crore in the bank divided by ₹15 lakh a month is **20 months of runway**.

Below twelve months, a company is fundraising whether it admits it or not. Eighteen months is the number most founders try to hold, because raising takes three to six months and you want to negotiate from a position where you can walk away.

## What a customer costs and what they're worth

**CAC** — *customer acquisition cost* — is total sales and marketing spend divided by new customers won.

Worked: ₹10 lakh of spend won 100 customers. CAC is **₹10,000**.

The honest version loads in the salaries of everyone in sales and marketing, not just the ad spend. Companies that quote ad-spend-only CAC are flattering themselves, and it's a fair thing to probe.

**LTV** — *lifetime value* — is what a customer is worth over their whole relationship. It's average revenue per customer, times gross margin, times how many months they stay. (Note: **margin**, not revenue. What a customer is worth is what you keep, not what they pay.)

Worked: ₹5,000 a month, 75% gross margin, 24-month average life.

LTV = 5,000 × 0.75 × 24 = **₹90,000**.

**LTV:CAC** is the ratio. Here, 90,000 ÷ 10,000 = **9:1**.

The rule of thumb — and it is a rule of thumb, not a law — is that **3:1 is healthy** and above 5:1 is strong. Below 3:1 you're buying revenue at close to cost. Wildly above it, oddly, can be a warning too: it often means you're underspending on growth in a market you could be taking faster.

## How fast you get your money back

**CAC payback** is the number of months to recover what you spent winning a customer.

CAC divided by (monthly revenue × gross margin).

Worked: ₹10,000 ÷ (₹5,000 × 0.75) = 10,000 ÷ 3,750 = **2.7 months**.

**Under twelve months is top-quartile.** This matters more than LTV:CAC for a company that's short of cash, because LTV is a projection stretching years out, while payback is a real date by which the money comes home. A startup with 20 months of runway and 18-month payback is in trouble regardless of how beautiful its LTV ratio looks.

## Whether customers stay, and whether they grow

**Churn** is customers lost divided by customers at the start of the period.

Two retention measures, and the difference is important.

**GRR** — *gross revenue retention* — is how much of last year's revenue you kept from those same customers, ignoring anything extra they bought. It can never exceed 100%, because it only counts losses.

**NRR** — *net revenue retention* — counts expansion too. Existing customers upgrading, adding seats, buying a second product. **NRR can exceed 100%.**

**Above 120% is top-quartile**, and it's the single number investors weight most heavily. Here's why. NRR of 120% means the revenue from last year's cohort grows 20% this year **with no new customers at all**. The business grows while standing still. That's the difference between a product people expand into and one they merely bought.

## The Rule of 40

A crude but durable test for a software business: **growth rate percentage plus profit margin percentage should be at least 40**.

Worked: growing 25% with a 20% margin gives 45. **Passes.**

The idea is that growth and profitability are exchangeable. Grow 60% and lose 20%, you pass. Grow 10% and make 35%, you pass. Do neither well and you fail.

It applies to software companies at reasonable scale. Applying it to a twelve-customer startup or an asset-heavy business is a category error, and knowing that is more useful than knowing the rule.

---

**Hold on to three things.** LTV:CAC above 3:1, CAC payback under twelve months, NRR above 120%. Payback matters more than LTV when cash is short, because payback is a date and LTV is a projection. And NRR above 100% means the business grows without winning anyone new.

# 7. How lending makes money

Lending has its own economics, and they don't behave like anything in Chapter 6. Get this chapter properly and the next one is easy.

## The basic equation

A lender borrows money at one rate and lends it at a higher one. The gap is the **spread**.

But the spread isn't profit, because some borrowers don't repay. So:

**Net margin = Yield − Cost of funds − Credit cost**

**Yield** is the rate you charge borrowers. **Cost of funds** is what you pay for the money you're lending. **Credit cost** is the percentage of the book you expect to lose.

Three numbers. Everything about lending sits inside them.

## The number nobody outside lending expects

Here's the thing that catches people: **credit cost is usually larger than you'd guess, and it arrives late.**

In India, unsecured lending — meaning loans with no collateral behind them — runs credit losses of roughly **4% to 8% of the book in normal conditions**. In bad years, pure-play unsecured lenders have seen it reach double digits.

Now set that against spreads. Non-bank lenders in India typically charge somewhere between 13% and 30% depending on risk, and pay maybe 12–14% for their own funding. So a gross spread of 4 or 5 points is common.

**A 5-point spread against a 6-point credit cost is a business that loses money.** And it will look profitable for the first two years, because that's how long it takes for the loans to go bad.

This is the single most important fact about lending, and it's why credit modelling is a real discipline rather than a formality.

## Why a lending business eats cash

Compare a software company to a lender.

For software, growth costs you sales and marketing. Once a customer is signed, they send you money every month. A great month means more cash in the bank.

For a balance-sheet lender, growth costs you sales and marketing **plus the entire principal of every loan you write**. You hand over ₹8 lakh today and get it back over the next sixty days. A record disbursal month is a **record cash outflow month**.

Read that again, because it inverts every instinct built up in Chapter 6. In lending, the better the month, the emptier the bank account.

And revenue arrives slowly — interest accrues over the life of the loan — while losses arrive later still. A fast-growing loan book always looks better than it is, because the good loans are counted immediately and the bad ones haven't gone bad yet.

## Two ways to run a lending business

**Structure A: lend off your own balance sheet.**

You need a licence. In India that means being an **NBFC** — a *non-banking financial company*, licensed by the RBI (the Reserve Bank of India, the central bank and financial regulator) to lend without being a bank. Registration requires roughly ₹2 crore of net owned funds as a floor, and far more in practice to lend at any scale.

Every rupee lent is a rupee of your capital consumed. You capture the full spread. You carry the full loss.

**Structure B: someone else's balance sheet, and you take a fee.**

You find the customer, assess their creditworthiness, and service the loan. A bank or NBFC actually funds it. You take a fee — say 1.5% of the amount disbursed.

In Indian regulation this role has a name: you are a **Lending Service Provider**, or **LSP**. Formally, an LSP is an agent of a **regulated entity** — a bank or NBFC — performing lending functions on its behalf: finding customers, supporting underwriting and pricing, servicing, monitoring, and collections.

You capture far less per rupee. But you deploy no capital, so your growth isn't limited by your equity. This is what "capital-light" means when a fintech says it.

## The catch, and it's a big one

Banks don't hand credit risk to a startup for free.

The usual arrangement is a **Default Loss Guarantee**, abbreviated **DLG** and also known as **FLDG**, for *first loss default guarantee*. The startup promises to cover the bank's losses up to a set percentage of the portfolio.

Indian regulation is specific about this, and the rules are worth knowing precisely because they shape what these companies can and cannot build:

**The guarantee is capped at 5% of the total amount disbursed.**

It must be backed by real money — cash, a fixed deposit with a lien marked on it, or a bank guarantee. Not a promise. Money that is locked and cannot be spent on anything else.

It must be invoked within 120 days of a loan going overdue.

And — this one has architectural consequences — **it is prohibited on credit cards and revolving credit facilities.**

## What the 5% cap actually costs

Worked. A startup wants to run ₹10 crore of disbursals through a bank partner.

Maximum guarantee: 5% of ₹10 crore = **₹50 lakh, locked in a fixed deposit.**

So "capital-light" is capital-*lighter*, not capital-free. You need roughly 5% of your loan book sitting in cash you can't touch. Grow the book from ₹10 crore to ₹50 crore and you need to find another ₹2 crore to lock up.

For an early-stage company, ₹2 crore is often more than a year of payroll.

## The asymmetry, which cuts both ways

Once cumulative losses hit the ₹50 lakh cap, the startup's liability stops. Everything beyond that reverts to the bank's balance sheet.

The bank knows this. Which is why bank partnerships take months, why banks scrutinise the underwriting model in detail, and why "structuring the bank partnership" is a genuine senior job rather than paperwork.

## The break-even that decides the deal

Here's the calculation that matters most, and it's simple.

If you earn a 1.5% fee on disbursals and you're guaranteeing losses up to 5%, at what loss rate do you stop making money?

**Break-even loss rate = Fee ÷ Amount disbursed**

Fee income on ₹10 crore at 1.5% is ₹15 lakh. Fifteen lakh divided by ₹10 crore is **1.5%**.

Above a 1.5% portfolio loss rate, the arrangement costs you money.

Now hold that against the benchmark from earlier in this chapter: unsecured lending loses **4% to 8%**.

A 1.5% break-even against a 4–8% benchmark isn't a margin of safety. It's a bet that whatever you're lending against behaves nothing like unsecured consumer credit. That bet can absolutely be right — a loan secured against a real invoice from a real corporate buyer is a very different animal. But it *is* a bet, and being able to name it as one is the difference between reading a term sheet and understanding it.

## Two more terms you'll need

**Delinquency buckets.** Loans overdue are sorted by how late they are: 30 days past due, 60, 90. The 30-day bucket is the earliest warning you get, and watching it move is how you catch a deteriorating book months before it shows up in the accounts.

**Collection efficiency.** Rupees actually collected divided by rupees due in the period. It's the number that tells you whether your underwriting is real or whether you've just been lending into a rising market.

---

**Hold on to three things.** Net margin is yield minus cost of funds minus credit cost, and credit cost is bigger and later than instinct suggests. In lending, a record month is a record cash outflow. And the guarantee cap is 5%, backed by locked cash, which is what puts a floor under "capital-light."

# 8. Nexo Money

Now apply Chapter 7.

## What it is

Nexo Money describes itself as **the credit-first financial operating system for Indian small and medium businesses** — SMBs. It's operated by a company called Finbraine Fintech Private Limited, based in Navi Mumbai.

Three products, sold as one platform.

**Corporate cards.** Physical and virtual cards issued to employees with spending limits built in — so the company controls spend at the moment of purchase rather than arguing about receipts a month later.

**Expense management.** Photographs of receipts are read automatically using **OCR** — *optical character recognition*, software that converts an image of text into actual text — matched to the card transactions, and pushed into the accounting system.

**Invoice factoring.** This is the credit product. The SMB has issued an invoice and is waiting 30 to 90 days to be paid. Nexo advances most of that money now.

Around the edges: UPI payouts for domestic vendors, zero-markup foreign exchange for international ones, and automated reconciliation of GST input tax credit.

## The three words that are the strategy

The website leads with three phrases: **Credit-First. Bank-Led. Capital-Efficient.** The careers page expands it — "delivered through the banks SMBs already trust. Built bank-led, not direct-to-business; capital-light, compliance-first, India-first."

Decode them and you have the whole business.

**Credit-first** means the wedge is lending, not software. The cards and expense tools are the surface. Credit is the product.

**Bank-led** means distribution runs through partner banks rather than Nexo acquiring SMBs one at a time.

**Capital-light** means Structure B from Chapter 7. Nexo is an LSP earning fees, not a balance-sheet lender.

## How the money comes in

Four possible lines, and the pricing isn't public, so what follows is illustrative arithmetic on realistic ranges — clearly labelled as such if you use it.

**Interchange on card spend.** When a card is used, the merchant pays a fee, and a portion of it — the **interchange** — flows back to whoever issued the card. Indian card programmes work off a merchant fee in the region of 0.5% to 2%, with the issuing bank taking a share.

**Subscription.** A monthly software fee per company.

**Factoring income.** A discount charge on each invoice advanced. This is the real line.

**FX and payouts.** Nexo advertises zero-markup FX, which by definition removes this as a revenue line. That tells you it's a customer-acquisition weapon, paid for by the other three.

## The arithmetic that tells you what stage the company is at

Nexo's careers page says: **twelve corporates and several thousand active users live.**

Now build the picture. Illustrative throughout.

Suppose those twelve corporates put ₹1 crore a month through the cards, at 1.2% interchange, of which Nexo keeps 40% after the issuing bank's share.

₹1 crore × 1.2% = ₹1.2 lakh gross a month. Nexo's 40% is **₹48,000 a month** — about ₹5.8 lakh a year.

Suppose the software subscription is ₹15,000 a month per corporate. Twelve of those is ₹1.8 lakh a month, or **₹21.6 lakh of ARR**.

Together: roughly **₹27 lakh a year**.

Now here's the fact that isn't illustrative. Nexo is currently hiring three people, with published cash bands of ₹18 lakh, ₹50 lakh and ₹20 lakh. That's **₹88 lakh of annual cash compensation for three hires.**

Three people cost more than three times what the software and cards produce.

**Which tells you the company is funded — or founder-funded — and is spending ahead of revenue to prove something other than the software.** That something is the credit engine.

This is the sharpest single observation available about Nexo, and it comes entirely from their own published numbers.

## The credit engine, with real arithmetic

An SMB has a ₹10 lakh invoice, payable in 60 days.

**If Nexo carried it themselves** (Structure A):

Advance 85% of face value, so ₹8.5 lakh goes out today. Charge 1.5% a month on the advance — 3% over 60 days — which is **₹25,500**.

Annualise that: 3% × (365 ÷ 60) = **18.25% a year**. That's a sensible rate for Indian non-bank lending, which runs 13% to 30% depending on risk.

Now subtract. Cost of funds, say 14%. Gross spread: **4.25%**.

Credit cost: this is invoice-backed and short-tenor, so call it a benign 2% rather than the 4–8% unsecured benchmark.

**Net spread: 2.25%.**

Deploy ₹10 crore and you gross ₹22.5 lakh of net margin — before paying a single salary.

That is why capital-light isn't a slogan. It's survival arithmetic.

**If the bank carries it** (Structure B):

Nexo sources, underwrites and services. The bank funds the ₹8.5 lakh. Nexo takes 1.5% of the disbursed amount: **₹12,750 per invoice.**

On ₹10 crore of annual disbursal: **₹15 lakh of fee income, against zero capital deployed.**

Less margin per rupee. Vastly better return on capital. And crucially, growth is never limited by Nexo's own equity.

## The constraint that shapes the product

Apply the DLG rules from Chapter 7.

On a ₹10 crore book, the maximum guarantee is ₹50 lakh, sitting in a locked fixed deposit. At ₹50 crore of book, it's ₹2.5 crore.

Break-even loss rate: 1.5% fee divided by the disbursed amount is **1.5%**. Above that, the arrangement is loss-making.

And here's the structural point most people miss.

**The guarantee is prohibited on credit cards and revolving credit facilities.**

So the card product cannot be de-risked for the bank the way factoring can. Which means — and this is an inference, not something Nexo has published — the card is most likely a prepaid or charge instrument settled from the SMB's own funds, with the actual credit living in the separately-structured factoring line.

If you get one technical question right about Nexo, make it that one.

## What the founder watches every week

TPV, or **total payment volume** — the rupees of card spend flowing through the platform, because it's the base that interchange is charged on.

Disbursals — rupees of credit advanced this week.

Active corporates — companies actually transacting, not merely signed. Twelve is the number that has to move.

Net burn and runway.

Delinquency by bucket, and collection efficiency. These two are the leading indicators of everything else.

And bank partnerships live — signed and integrated, not in discussion. This is the distribution bottleneck.

## Where the model breaks

**Regulation moving underneath the product.** This isn't hypothetical in Indian fintech; it's the base case. In June 2022 the RBI barred non-bank lenders from loading credit lines onto prepaid instruments, and a well-funded card startup lost its entire operating architecture overnight — growth to zero, funding round frozen, competitors quietly shut. That's precisely the structure a credit-plus-card fintech reaches for.

And the ground is moving again. The RBI released a draft revision of the prepaid instrument rules in April 2026, with proposals including a prohibition on loading general-purpose prepaid instruments with credit cards. Commentary reads the direction as *same activity, same risk, same regulation* — the end of lighter-touch treatment for fintechs.

**Credit losses arriving after growth.** An SMB book underwritten on GST filings and bank statements rather than collateral will be tested by one downturn. And the fee model requires sustaining disbursal volume, because earnings depend on it — which is a treadmill.

**Bank dependency.** Bank-led distribution means bank-paced everything. A partner that de-prioritises the programme stops the company. And the bank can always ask for more guarantee cover, which is cash.

## Who else is in this market

**Razorpay** is payments-first with enormous distribution, adding business banking on top. **Happay**, now owned by CRED, does expense management and corporate cards, weighted to larger enterprises. **Zoho** owns the SMB accounting workflow and is cheap and sticky. **Kodo, Volopay and Karbon** are the direct comparables — SMB cards plus spend management.

And there's a regulated route for invoice discounting already: **TReDS**, the *trade receivables discounting system*, an RBI-sanctioned platform where small businesses auction their unpaid invoices to financiers. It's both a competitor and a potential channel.

Finally, the banks themselves. Which is the interesting question about the strategy: **is "bank-led" a moat, or a dependency?** That's a good thing to ask a founder.

## What stage this actually is

Evidence pointing early: the website's main call to action is *Request Early Access* — a waitlist, not a signup. The site is built on Wix, careers on a Netlify subdomain. The company calls all three open roles "founding-era" and says the architectural choices are still being made.

Evidence pointing real: twelve corporates and several thousand users live. A six-month engineering fellowship starting August 2026 describing work on credit decisioning, regulatory reporting, collections and fraud, across a lending stack spanning origination, underwriting, KYC, credit bureaux, disbursal and collections. That's a production lending system being described, not a landing page. And ₹88 lakh of committed annual cash comp.

Reading it honestly: **pre-Series-A, post-first-revenue.** Product publicly in early access while a small live cohort runs in production. Spending ahead of revenue on the bet that the credit engine works.

---

**Hold on to three things.** Cards and software are the surface; credit is the business, and Nexo is an LSP earning fees rather than a balance-sheet lender. The 5% guarantee cap, backed by locked cash and banned on revolving credit, shapes the entire product architecture. And three hires cost more than three times what the visible revenue produces, which tells you what they're really betting on.

# 9. How asset businesses make money

Different animal entirely. Everything in Chapter 6 assumed low marginal cost — one more software customer costs you nearly nothing. Asset businesses are the opposite: you spend a large sum upfront, and then you spend years earning it back.

## The one number that matters

**Utilisation** is actual output sold divided by theoretical maximum output.

A machine that could produce 1,440 units a day and sells 144 is running at 10% utilisation. That's it. That's the number.

(The equivalent term for power generation is **capacity factor** — actual electricity produced divided by nameplate capacity over a period. Same idea, different industry.)

Everything about an asset business is downstream of utilisation, and here's why it matters more than intuition suggests.

## Fixed-cost leverage, demonstrated

The reason utilisation dominates is that **fixed costs don't move with volume**. Rent is rent whether you sell one unit or a thousand. So every extra unit sold drops almost entirely into profit.

Here is the demonstration, and I want you to feel the size of it. Take an electric vehicle charger — the example generalises to any asset.

A 60-kilowatt fast charger. All in — hardware, civil work, cabling, the electrical connection, commissioning — call it **₹15 lakh of capex**.

You buy electricity from the state distribution company (a **DISCOM**) at a concessional rate for EV charging: about **₹6.50 per kilowatt-hour**. A **kilowatt-hour**, or **kWh**, is the unit electricity is sold in.

You sell charging at about **₹18 per kWh**.

**Gross margin: ₹11.50 per kWh.**

Theoretical maximum output: 60 kW running 24 hours = **1,440 kWh a day**.

Fixed monthly costs: rent ₹15,000, maintenance and site operations ₹10,000, payment and network fees ₹3,000. **₹28,000 a month.**

**At 10% utilisation:**

144 kWh a day × ₹11.50 = ₹1,656 a day, or ₹49,680 a month.

Less ₹28,000 of fixed costs: **₹21,680 a month of net profit.**

Payback: ₹15,00,000 ÷ ₹21,680 = **69 months. Nearly six years.** The site doesn't work.

**At 20% utilisation:**

288 kWh a day × ₹11.50 = ₹3,312 a day, or ₹99,360 a month.

Less the same ₹28,000: **₹71,360 a month.**

Payback: ₹15,00,000 ÷ ₹71,360 = **21 months.** The site works.

**Utilisation doubled. Net profit rose 3.3 times. Payback fell by four years.**

That non-linearity is the entire business. It's why the same asset, in two locations a kilometre apart, can be a good investment and a catastrophe.

## What this means for how you build

Three consequences follow, and they're the whole strategic playbook for asset businesses.

**First: site selection is the business.** Not the technology, not the app, not the brand. Once the capex is in the ground you cannot fix a bad location. You can only write it off.

**Second: convert fixed costs to variable wherever you can.** If instead of paying ₹15,000 rent you give the landlord a share of revenue, a low-utilisation ramp becomes survivable rather than fatal. You earn less per unit at high volume and you don't die at low volume. When utilisation is unproven, that's the right trade every time.

**Third: contracted demand beats hoped-for demand.** A guaranteed customer — someone who commits to volume in advance — is worth more than the margin you give up to get them, because it moves you from the 10% scenario to the 20% scenario. In energy this counterparty is called an **off-taker**, and the risk that they stop buying or paying is **off-taker risk**.

## Why EBITDA lies here

Return to Chapter 1's warning.

The 10% utilisation site generates ₹49,680 a month of gross margin. Strip out depreciation and it can post respectable EBITDA. But it will not recover ₹15 lakh for six years, and by then the hardware needs replacing.

**In an asset business, look at payback per site and free cash flow. Never EBITDA alone.**

## The structure that lets a small company build big

There's a well-established answer to "I have no capital but I want to own assets," and it's worth knowing because it appears everywhere in Indian energy.

In rooftop solar, the two models are called **CAPEX** and **OPEX**.

Under **CAPEX**, the customer buys and owns the system outright.

Under **OPEX** — also called the **RESCO** model, for *renewable energy service company* — the developer owns the equipment, installs it on the customer's roof at zero upfront cost, and sells the electricity produced under a **PPA**, a *power purchase agreement*, typically running 20 to 25 years at a fixed rate per unit.

The customer gets cheaper power with no capital outlay. The developer gets a long-dated contracted revenue stream — which is to say, guaranteed utilisation.

Apply the same shape to any asset: someone else owns the hardware, you own the customer relationship and the operating contract. **That's how a company with a small balance sheet builds a large asset base.**

## Some vocabulary you'll meet

**LCOE** — *levelized cost of energy* — is the all-in cost per unit across an asset's whole life, including capex, financing and maintenance. It's how you compare a solar plant against a diesel generator on equal terms.

**O&M** — *operations and maintenance* — the recurring cost of keeping the asset working.

**Project IRR** — the internal rate of return on the site's capex over its life. Same IRR from Chapter 4, applied to a piece of infrastructure rather than a company.

**Uptime** — the share of time the asset actually works. Which sounds trivial and is not: an asset that's broken has zero utilisation, and in Indian EV charging roughly 18% of installed stations were non-functional as of early 2026.

---

**Hold on to three things.** Utilisation is the business — 10% never pays back, 20% pays back in under two years, and the gap is fixed costs not moving with volume. Convert fixed costs to variable when demand is unproven. And EBITDA flatters asset businesses; look at payback per site.

# 10. EnerZ

Now apply Chapter 9. But start with an honest caveat: **the public record on EnerZ is thin.** Two registered companies, a startup-registry entry, a handful of directory listings. No funding announcements, no press, no readable product page. What follows separates what's documented from what's reasonable inference.

## What's on the record

**ENERZ Charge Infra Private Limited** was incorporated on 8 August 2024 in Mulanthuruthy, Ernakulam, Kerala. Kurian George is the founder. Its registered description is renewable energy charging stations and charging infrastructure — public, private and commercial.

**ENERZ Fleet Solutions Private Limited** was incorporated the same day. The sequential registration numbers confirm it.

**Both companies have ₹1,00,000 of paid-up capital.** One lakh. Each.

Separately, a business directory describes EnerZ as a four-employee company in Kochi aiming to become India's leading electric ride-sharing service.

## The apparent contradiction, resolved

One source says charging infrastructure. Another says electric ride-sharing.

These aren't contradictory. They're two arms of one plan, and the company structure confirms it: **Charge Infra builds the charging; Fleet Solutions runs the vehicles.**

And once you've read Chapter 9, you know exactly why someone would do that.

**A captive fleet is guaranteed utilisation.** If you own the cars and you own the chargers, you're not hoping strangers turn up. You're not in the 10% scenario. You're in the 20% scenario by construction.

That's not a coincidence of corporate structure. That's the strategy.

## The market, in the numbers that matter

India crossed **10 million registered electric vehicles** in mid-2026. EV penetration — the share of new vehicle sales that are electric — rose from under 1% in 2020 to **around 8%** by early 2026, and monthly retail figures crossed 12% for the first time in June 2026.

Charging has not kept up. Public charging stations number **somewhere under 50,000 nationally** — and I'd flag that official sources disagree by roughly two-to-one on the exact figure, most likely because some count individual charging points and others count sites. Whichever figure you take, the ratio is roughly **one public charger for every 200 EVs**, against an international benchmark closer to one per ten.

And **about 18% of installed stations were non-operational** as of early 2026. In some networks the failure rate has been reported far higher. Uptime is the industry's real problem, not coverage.

Kerala, where EnerZ is based, has among the highest state-level EV penetration in India — a genuine tailwind.

## The economics, which you already know

Chapter 9's worked example *was* an EV charger. Buy at ₹6.50, sell at ₹18, margin ₹11.50 per kWh. ₹15 lakh of capex. Ten percent utilisation gives 69-month payback; twenty percent gives 21 months.

Industry sources put typical breakeven at **18 to 36 months** for a well-located station, which brackets that arithmetic neatly. Target utilisation is generally quoted at 30–50%; 25% is described as thin.

Two levers can transform a marginal site.

**Advertising.** High-visibility urban sites earn ₹10,000 to ₹50,000 a month from display advertising. On the 10%-utilisation site netting ₹21,680, that more than doubles net profit — with no additional capex. It's the fastest available lever and it's routinely ignored.

**Fleet subscription contracts.** A fixed monthly fee for guaranteed access. Described in the trade as the most underused profit strategy among new Indian operators. It converts volatile retail demand into contracted demand — which is Chapter 9's third principle, and again, exactly what owning a fleet achieves.

## The government money, and the catch

The central **PM E-DRIVE** scheme subsidises up to **50% of fast-charger capex**, with about ₹2,000 crore allocated for charging infrastructure.

Halve the capex on the 10%-utilisation site and payback falls from 69 months to about 35. Subsidy converts a dead site into a marginal one. It does not rescue a bad location.

Two conditions attached: **95% uptime**, and compliance with **OCPP** — the *open charge point protocol*, the open standard that lets chargers communicate with management software. Fail either and the subsidy is at risk.

And the catch that matters most: **as of March 2026, essentially none of that ₹2,000 crore had actually been spent.** Announced subsidy is not received subsidy. Never underwrite a site on it.

State schemes layer on top — Maharashtra waives electricity duty, Karnataka adds a capex grant, Tamil Nadu mandates EV-ready parking in new commercial buildings. Together these are credited with cutting typical payback from around seven years to under four on high-traffic corridors.

GST on EVs was cut from 12% to 5%, and on chargers from 18% to 5%.

## The bottleneck nobody expects

It isn't the hardware. It's the electricity connection.

You need a DISCOM connection at the concessional EV tariff. And **for electrical loads above 100 kVA** — kilovolt-ampere, a measure of electrical capacity — **the DISCOM must conduct a feasibility study**, which determines whether the cost of upgrading the local grid falls on you or on the utility.

That allocation can change a project's return entirely. A single 60 kW charger sits under the threshold. A four-bay hub does not. **This is why hubs stall and single chargers don't.**

You also need Electrical Inspector sign-off, a Fire Safety no-objection certificate, and BIS-certified hardware.

## Where this model breaks

**Utilisation risk, which is really site-selection risk.** The Chapter 9 arithmetic is unforgiving, and you cannot fix a bad site after the capex is in the ground.

**Capital intensity against a ₹1 lakh balance sheet.** One fast charger costs fifteen times the company's entire paid-up capital. **EnerZ cannot self-fund a network.** It needs external funding, a franchise structure, or an asset-light model where someone else owns the hardware. This is the most important structural fact about the company, and it comes straight off the public register.

**Price compression.** The central government has recommended guideline tariffs far below what operators actually charge — ₹4 per unit for slow charging, ₹7 for fast. Not enforced today, but a clear signal of direction. If the sell price is pushed toward ₹10 while input stays at ₹6.50, margin falls from ₹11.50 to ₹3.50 and the good site's payback stretches from 21 months to over six years. **Tariff regulation is existential for a thin-margin business.**

**Competing against balance sheets.** Tata Power, IndianOil, BPCL and Jio-BP have thousands of sites and effectively unlimited capital. A four-person Kerala company doesn't win a land grab. It wins by owning demand.

## What stage this is

Two companies incorporated the same day with ₹1 lakh each. Four-person directory headcount. No discoverable funding, no press, no product page. Against that: a founder with a genuine multi-venture history, a state startup registry entry, an active senior hire, and a coherent two-entity strategy.

Reading it honestly: **pre-seed or seed. Likely pre-revenue or barely revenue. Building a plan rather than scaling one.**

Which means a senior operator joining here would be closer to a founding operator than a strategic advisor. That's not a criticism. It's just a different job, and worth knowing which one you're walking into.

---

**Hold on to three things.** Charging plus fleet is a captive-demand structure, and that's the strategy, not a coincidence. ₹1 lakh of paid-up capital against ₹15 lakh per charger means the model needs outside capital or an asset-light structure. And the public record is thin and internally inconsistent — say so rather than pretending to know.

# 11. Where the two meet

Two businesses, one founder, and — once you look properly — one idea.

## The idea

**Somebody needs to underwrite a small business, and nobody has good data on them.**

That's the problem in both companies.

An Indian SMB wanting a working-capital loan has thin financials, no collateral, and a credit history that mostly doesn't exist. A bank looks at it and declines, not out of malice but because it genuinely cannot price the risk.

An EV fleet operator is the same borrower with a different logo. Thin books, no assets a bank wants, revenue that lives inside a payments app.

## The connection

Here's the part worth understanding properly.

**The charging platform generates exactly the data that would make the fleet operator underwritable.**

How much energy did they draw last month? Is it growing? Do they charge at consistent times, suggesting consistent utilisation? Did they pay on time? A charging network sees a fleet operator's actual business activity in real time — better data than a bank could ever get from filed accounts.

That's an **embedded lending** thesis: the platform that serves the customer operationally is the platform best placed to lend to them, because it can see things a lender cannot.

And it runs the other way too. A charging network needs financing — for hardware, for site build-out, and for the small businesses that host or use it. Those hosts are exactly Nexo's customers.

## The two words on the founder's profile

Kurian George's public headline lists **impact financing** and **CBDC**. Both are worth understanding, and one of them connects to something you already know.

**Impact financing** means investing for measurable social or environmental outcomes alongside a financial return.

The technique that makes it work is **blended finance**: concessional capital — money from a government, a development bank or a philanthropic foundation that accepts a below-market return — deployed to de-risk a deal so that ordinary commercial investors will participate.

The main instrument is a **first-loss tranche**. Someone junior agrees to absorb the first slice of losses. Everyone senior to them is protected, so they lend at rates they otherwise wouldn't. Typical leverage runs three to ten times — a $2 million first-loss layer can mobilise $10 million of private capital.

Now read that against Chapter 7.

**A first-loss tranche in blended finance and a Default Loss Guarantee in Indian digital lending are structurally the same instrument.** Someone junior absorbs the first losses so someone senior will lend at all. The RBI caps DLG at 5%. A blended-finance first-loss layer might be 10 or 20%. Same mechanism, different regulator, different name.

Being able to say that one sentence is worth more than a page of impact-finance vocabulary.

**CBDC** stands for *central bank digital currency* — sovereign digital money issued directly by the central bank. Not a bank deposit, not private cryptocurrency. India's version is the **e-rupee**, in pilot since late 2022 and still tiny next to UPI.

The word that matters is **programmable** — money that can only be spent on a defined purpose. India has been piloting exactly this for subsidy transfers, where the money can only be used at authorised outlets.

Connect it: for a subsidised charging network or a concessional green loan, programmability means a subsidy can be **traced to its outcome** rather than audited after the fact. That's the plausible reason those three interests sit on one profile — and it's worth offering as a hypothesis, not asserting as a claim about someone's strategy.

## What sits across both

If you were the person connecting these two businesses, the valuable work is:

The economics of each partnership — bank agreements on one side, site and host agreements on the other.

The operating rhythm that keeps both moving.

And the genuinely interesting piece: **one underwriting view of the small-business customer**, whether they're swiping a corporate card or hosting a charger.

---

**Hold on to three things.** Both businesses are the same problem — underwriting a small business nobody has data on. The charging platform generates the data that makes an unbankable fleet operator bankable. And a blended-finance first-loss tranche is a DLG under a different name.

# 12. Thinking, then writing

Two skills. Break the problem down; say the answer first. Everything else is decoration.

## Breaking a problem down

**MECE** — pronounced "mee-see," standing for *mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive* — means splitting a problem into parts that don't overlap and together cover everything.

Splitting customers into "new" and "returning" is MECE. Splitting them into "new," "returning" and "enterprise" is not, because an enterprise customer is also either new or returning. That overlap will produce double-counting and a wrong answer.

An **issue tree** is what you get when you keep doing it — the big question at the top, sub-questions beneath, sub-sub-questions beneath those.

The most useful trick: **break things down multiplicatively wherever you can**, because it makes each factor separately measurable.

*Revenue = Customers × Average revenue per customer.*
*Sign-ups = Traffic × Conversion rate.*
*Charging revenue = Utilisation × Margin per unit.*

Once a problem is a product of factors, you can go and measure each factor and find where the number actually broke.

## Testing, not exploring

The novice approach is to gather all available data and see what emerges. It's thorough, slow, and often produces nothing.

**Hypothesis-driven** means starting with the answer you think is most likely and going to test it. If you're right, you've saved a week. If you're wrong, the test tells you where to look next.

Worked. *Our sign-ups have gone flat. Why?*

Sign-ups = Traffic × Conversion.

Traffic splits by channel: referral, direct, content, outbound. Conversion splits by funnel stage: landing page, registration, identity verification, first transaction.

Now form the hypothesis rather than measuring everything. Identity verification is the only stage that involves a regulated third party and can fail for reasons entirely outside our control. **That's probably where the drop is.**

Go and measure that stage first. If 40% of registrations are failing verification, you've found it in an afternoon — and no amount of additional marketing spend would have fixed it.

## Always end with a threshold

The single biggest difference between a junior answer and a senior one:

A junior answer ends with *"it depends on the data."*

A senior answer ends with *"here's the number that decides it, and here's what we do on each side."*

Worked. *Should we build chargers in a new city?*

Frame it three ways: market, economics, risk. Market is local EV registrations, dwell time at candidate sites, competing chargers nearby. Economics is projected utilisation times ₹11.50 margin, less fixed costs, against ₹15 lakh of capex. Risk is the DISCOM connection timeline, whether the load crosses 100 kVA, land terms, and tariff regulation.

Then the recommendation, and this is the part that matters:

*Pilot three sites on revenue-share land terms. Scale only where a site clears 15% utilisation by month four. Below that, exit — the arithmetic says a 10% site never pays back.*

A decision rule and a number. Not "it depends."

## Saying it: answer first

The **pyramid principle** — the idea is Barbara Minto's, from her time at McKinsey — says: state your answer first, then the grouped arguments that support it, then the detail beneath each.

The military short form is **BLUF** — *bottom line up front*.

Almost everyone does the opposite. They build up: here's the context, here's what we looked at, here's what we found, and therefore. That's how you *did* the work, so it feels natural to present it that way.

It's wrong, because a senior person will interrupt at sentence three and you'll never reach the point.

**Lead with the conclusion. Every time.**

## The one-page memo

Five parts, in order.

**The recommendation.** One sentence. What we should do.

**Why.** Three lines. The reasoning, not the process.

**The trade-off.** What we're giving up, stated honestly. Including it makes the recommendation more credible, not less — a recommendation with no cost is a recommendation nobody believes.

**The real risk.** Not the convenient one. This is the single fastest way to be trusted in a room, and almost nobody does it.

**The ask.** Something specific, with an owner and a date.

Here's one, written on a real question from Chapter 8.

> **Recommendation:** Prioritise invoice factoring this quarter. Defer the FX product by one quarter.
>
> **Why:** Factoring addresses the working-capital gap that is the reason SMBs take our call at all, and it's the only line with meaningful unit economics — cards at our current volume produce roughly ₹48,000 a month against a fixed cost base many times that. Eight of our twelve corporates have asked for factoring. FX has lower stated demand, a heavier compliance load, and we've advertised it at zero markup, so it's an acquisition feature rather than a revenue line.
>
> **Trade-off:** Two corporates need FX now. We handle them manually for one quarter — roughly four hours a week of operations time.
>
> **The real risk:** Not the FX delay. It's that factoring pulls us deeper into credit exposure before the bank partnership is fully papered. If the partnership slips, we're carrying risk we hadn't planned to carry.
>
> **Ask:** Move two engineers from the FX integration to the factoring underwriting flow. Decision needed by Friday to make the August cohort.

Two hundred words. Leads with the call. Uses the company's own numbers. Names the real risk rather than the comfortable one. Ends with a specific ask and a date.

That's the format. It works for almost everything.

---

**Hold on to three things.** Break problems down multiplicatively so each factor is measurable. End every analysis with a threshold number and a decision rule. And name the real risk, not the comfortable one.

# 13. The numbers to actually memorise

Everything else in this document you can reason your way back to. These you should be able to produce instantly.

## Rules of thumb

**LTV:CAC of 3:1** is the health line. Above 5:1 is strong.

**CAC payback under 12 months** is top-quartile. Under 6 is excellent.

**NRR above 100%** is the bar. **Above 120%** is top-quartile, and it means the business grows without a single new customer.

**Rule of 40** — growth percentage plus profit margin percentage should reach 40.

**18 months of runway** is what a founder tries to hold, because raising takes three to six.

**3× MOIC over 5 years is roughly a 25% IRR.**

**A 4-year vest with a 1-year cliff** is the standard for equity.

## Lending

**Unsecured credit losses run 4–8%** of the book in normal conditions in India, higher in bad years.

**Indian non-bank lending rates run 13–30%**, depending on risk.

**The default loss guarantee is capped at 5%** of the disbursed portfolio, must be backed by locked cash, invoked within 120 days, and **is prohibited on credit cards and revolving credit**.

**Break-even loss rate = fee ÷ amount disbursed.** On a 1.5% fee, that's 1.5%.

## Charging

**Buy at ₹6.50, sell at ₹18. Margin ₹11.50 per kWh.**

**₹15 lakh capex** for a 60 kW fast charger, all in.

**10% utilisation → 69-month payback. 20% → 21 months.** This is the pair to have on instant recall; it demonstrates fixed-cost leverage better than any explanation.

**Above 100 kVA**, the DISCOM feasibility study is mandatory, and it decides who pays for the grid upgrade.

**PM E-DRIVE covers up to 50% of fast-charger capex**, conditional on 95% uptime — but almost none of the allocation had been spent as of March 2026.

## The two companies

**Nexo: twelve corporates live**, and **₹88 lakh of annual cash comp across three open roles.** The gap between those two facts is the story.

**EnerZ: ₹1 lakh of paid-up capital, per entity.** Against ₹15 lakh per charger.

## India, roughly

**10 million registered EVs. Around 8% EV penetration.** Fewer than 50,000 public chargers, roughly one per 200 vehicles, with about 18% of them non-functional.

That's the list. Around thirty numbers. Everything else you derive.

**EBIT** — earnings before interest and tax; operating profit.

**Equity risk premium** — the extra return investors demand for shares over government bonds.
